# Tense & Aspect Teaching — Best Known Way for Children & ESL Learners, esp. Chinese Speakers

## Summary

**There is no single “best” product, but there is a convergent best model — and it is not the 3×4 grid alone.** Across linguistics (Comrie, Smith, Reichenbach), SLA experiments (Concept-Based Instruction; a 2026 compositional-instruction experiment), and the strongest learner materials (Murphy, Seonaid/Perfect English Grammar, EnglishPage, Lingolia, Reasoning School's own later sections), the winning design separates **three independent decisions** and makes each one *manipulable*:

1. **Where is the reference point R?** (Reichenbach's E–R–S: event time, reference/“camera-standpoint” time, speech time) — not just “when did it happen?”
2. **How does the speaker *view* the event from R?** (aspect as camera viewpoint: whole event from outside, zoomed-inside process, or looking back at it with its result/consequence at R)
3. **What kind of event is it lexically?** (state / activity / accomplishment / achievement — endpoint or not), because learners acquire tense-aspect prototype-by-prototype (Aspect Hypothesis).

The 3×4 grid is the best-known **form-building engine** for those decisions (Reasoning School's block-chaining “recipe” is the clearest version found, verified live), and the worst-known **meaning curriculum** if it is the centrepiece: it teaches 12 (in China, 16) symmetric, equally-weighted labels, hides R, treats “future” as a tense parallel to past, and invites the Chinese learner's core misconception — perfect = 完成 = “finished” (了).

**Verdict: MODIFY — demote, don't delete.** Keep the grid as the *map / recipe card / end-of-unit summary* the learner can always open. Replace the centrepiece with a **scene machine**: one event bar on a timeline, a draggable **NOW** and a draggable **story-camera (R)**, a **viewpoint zoom switch**, a **result-at-R photo**, and a **story player** in which foreground cards advance the clock and background cards don't. Priority upgrades and traps are listed in §6–§8.

Confidence: high on the linguistic/SLA synthesis (multiple independent primary sources agree); medium on product comparisons (several Chinese apps' tense units are behind paywalls and could not be verified — see Could Not Verify).

## Findings

### 1. Visual models of tense/aspect

#### 1.1 Classic timeline diagrams — necessary base layer, known limits
What learner sees: a horizontal line, past left / now centre / future right; **X** = point event, **wiggly/segment line** = ongoing, **arrow to now** = perfect duration. This is the EnglishPage convention, praised and reproduced by Principia's teaching centre: simple present needs *repeated Xs* for habits and a *line through all time* for general truths — i.e. one form needs several different timelines.
- EnglishPage, *Present Perfect* — `https://www.englishpage.com/verbpage/presentperfect.html` — index, read 2026-11-01. Its three GIF diagrams are the most copyable classic: (a) Xs scattered at *unspecified* times before now (experience), (b) the same Xs inside a *limited window* (“in the last year” — explicitly contrasted with finished “last year”), (c) an arrow from a past start up to now (duration, non-continuous verbs).
- Principia discussion of EnglishPage chart — `http://content.principia.edu/teaching-excellence/drawing-conclusions-about-tense-part-i/` — index.
- Effectiveness: computer-based timeline media with 251 Indonesian senior-high students, 6 tense modules, showed a significant pre→post gain and positive teacher response — Teopilus et al., `https://media.neliti.com/media/publications/375836-using-computer-based-timeline-media-to-t-9d4088ae.pdf` — index. Weakness: no strong control group reported in the abstract, and gains were on tense *tests*, not discourse use.
- **Limits (documented in the materials themselves):** a point-X cannot show habit, state, or “unfinished time”; simple present is *not* “at now”; present perfect events sit at the same timeline place as simple past events — the difference is viewpoint/relevance, which a position-only diagram cannot encode. Any timeline-only app therefore *must* add a second channel (viewpoint/result) or it teaches “position = tense”.

#### 1.2 Reichenbach E–R–S — the rigorous model, needs kid translation
Reichenbach (1947): tense needs three points — **S** speech point, **E** event point, **R** reference point (the time the sentence is *about / viewed from*, set by context/story). Past perfect = E—R—S; present perfect = E—S,R (R coincides with S); future perfect = S—E—R / S,R—E variants. Source text via Slideshare: `https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/reichenbach-tenses-of-verbs1947pdf/263641875` — index.
The 2026 AJAL paper restates the same idea in teachable form as Tr (time referred to) vs To (time of orientation/utterance): past = Tr < To, present = Tr = To, future = Tr > To — `http://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1511004.pdf` — index, read 2026-11-01.
- **Learner-facing versions found:** Reasoning School's story timeline is an *implicit* R-lesson — “Paati reached the station” (simple past) is named “our reference point”, “the train had left” is *before that reference point* (see §2.1). No child-facing product found that lets learners **drag R independently of S and E** — this is the clearest white space / upgrade opportunity. Letters E/R/S alone are too abstract for primary learners; the translatable version is three characters/objects: **Event** (what happened), **Camera/Story-Now** (where we're standing to look), **Me-Now** (real now).
- Limit: Reichenbach's E is a *point*; durative events need bars/intervals, and progressive/perfect-progressive need an interval R inside/around the event bar — a point-only ERS widget will break exactly on the aspects children struggle with.

#### 1.3 Comrie + Smith: aspect as viewpoint / camera lens — the best meaning definition
- Comrie (1976:3): aspects are “different ways of **viewing the internal temporal constituency** of a situation”; perfective = view as a single whole (beginning/middle/end rolled into one), imperfective = pay attention to internal structure / view from inside. Via Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy: `https://plato.stanford.edu/archIves/win2016/entries/tense-aspect/` — index. Same source gives Vendler's lexical classes (states *know*, activities *run*, accomplishments *cross the street*, achievements *recognise*) and notes states/achievements resist progressive.
- Smith's camera metaphor, via Kroeger/LibreTexts §20.4: “Aspectual viewpoints present situations with a particular perspective or focus, **rather like the focus of a camera lens**… perfective = wide-angle (situation fits inside the frame); imperfective/progressive = zoom/close-up (only part visible, situation extends beyond frame edges)” — `https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Linguistics/Analyzing_Meaning_-_An_Introduction_to_Semantics_and_Pragmatics_(Kroeger)/20:_Aspect_and_Aktionsart/20.04:_Grammatical_Aspect_(_viewpoint_aspect)` — index.
- Lingolia is the best *learner-facing* implementation of this wording: progressive = “you **view** an action as ongoing, incomplete or temporary”, perfect = “you **view** a completed action as **connected to a later time**”, result emphasised — `https://english.lingolia.com/en/grammar/tenses/timeline` — index (see §2.2).
- ⚠️ Trap in the literature: some sources swap the lens labels (wide for progressive, narrow for simple). Fix one convention and never vary it: **wide/outside = simple (whole event, endpoints visible); zoom/inside = progressive (endpoints out of frame); look-back = perfect (event behind R, result in frame at R).**

#### 1.5 Physical / single-protagonist metaphors (incl. Chinese phenomenon 英语兔）
- **英语兔 (English Rabbit) — the dominant Chinese explainer model.** Time (now/past/future/past-future) × State （一般/进行/完成/完成进行） = **16**, one rabbit eating carrots in *every* cell: progressive = rabbit in the process of eating (arrow), perfect = carrots eaten — in the belly, full; perfect-progressive = ate some, still to continue — `https://www.codeplayer.org/Wiki/English/16%E7%A7%8D%E6%97%B6%E6%80%81%E8%AF%A6%E8%A7%A3.html`, notes at `https://blog.csdn.net/jsl123x/article/details/134104471` — index. **Lesson for design:** Chinese learners demonstrably love (a) *one protagonist held constant* while only time/state change (exactly Lingolia's cake and Reasoning's rangoli/paint), and (b) a *visible state of the object* (carrots partly eaten). **Trap:** its perfect icon — *all eaten, in the belly* — hard-codes perfect = completion, which is wrong for unfinished perfect (“I have lived here for years”, “I've known her since 2020”) and feeds the 了-transfer error (§3.3).
- **Tense train / sentence train, string, tube:** no rigorous, widely-adopted “tense train” model was found; “sentence train” (subject = engine, predicate = carriages) is a word-order metaphor, not tense-aspect — `https://lilbutmightyenglish.com/blog/the-sentence-train/` — index. Classroom “time machine” props (cardboard box, sort sentences into PAST/PRESENT/FUTURE baskets) teach only the 3-way time sort — e.g. `https://learningcorner.co/lesson/24495` — index. Treat physical metaphors as hooks, not models.

### 2. Interactive products — what learners actually *do*

#### 2.1 Reasoning School — Tense Time Machine — VERIFIED LIVE
URL: `https://www.reasoning.school/learn/english/foundation-03.html` — **verified live in research window, 2026-11-02** (full detail in notes/sources.md).
- **Model:** §F3.3 states the grid explicitly (“When? 3 choices. How do we see the action? 4 choices. 3×4=12”). Widget observed live: a **discrete 3-position time slider** (not a continuous scrub), **4 aspect buttons**, a timeline graphic, and one held-constant event (Meera paints a rangoli). Sentence and form label update per cell.
- **Its best, genuinely original piece — the recipe / block chain:** “Build the verb from left to right. **Time goes on the first helper. Continuous adds be + -ing. Perfect adds have + V3.**” Then a step-by-step builder for *will have been painting*: will → next block must be V1 → have → next must be V3 → been → be wants -ing → painting. “Every block decides the shape of the next one.” This is the strongest form-building interaction found anywhere in this research.
- **Crucially, the page itself corrects its own grid** in the sections around the machine: F3.4 stative “-ing trap” (*I am knowing* ✗), present perfect as “the past that still matters” + since/for + finished-time ban; **F3.5 story model** — simple past *moves the story forward*, past continuous *paints background*, past perfect *jumps further back*, with a two-events-on-one-timeline animation (train had left → Paati reached = reference point); **F3.6 “English has no single future form; it borrows”** — will / going to / present continuous / simple present by meaning, + present (not will) after when/if.
- **Limitations:** the machine widget itself has (a) no movable reference point separate from the event/now — past perfect in the machine is just a cell, its *meaning* is only taught later in the story section; (b) no viewpoint/zoom representation — aspect is buttons + labels; (c) one event type only (an activity, *paint*) — no states/achievements, so learners can't discover why **is knowing* fails or why perfect-progressive of an achievement is odd; (d) slider snaps to 3 labelled zones, reinforcing “time = 3 boxes”.

#### 2.2 Lingolia — interactive tense timeline — the closest competitor
URL: `https://english.lingolia.com/en/grammar/tenses/timeline` — index, read 2026-11-01.
- **Model/does:** click a tense *or an aspect row*; four aspect illustrations (simple / progressive / perfect / perfect-progressive SVGs) shown across past/present/future with **the same event** (“bake a cake”) in all 12 cells, each glossed in viewpoint language (§1.3) plus a usage note. Separate *Tense Comparison* pages teach grounding explicitly: simple past = “completed, sequential… **main narrative form**”, past progressive = “**establishes background and sets the scene**” — `https://english.lingolia.com/en/grammar/tense-comparison/simple-past-past-progressive` — index.
- **Best:** aspect-row reading (same viewpoint, three times) — the grid read *by rows*, which most grid teaching never does. **Limit:** click-to-reveal, not drag/scrub; no R, no result state, no learner-placed events; paywalled exercises.

#### 2.3 TenseBuilder (Mobile Education Store) — best “watch → freeze → do” loop for young children
URLs: `https://tensebuilder.appstor.io/`, review `https://lessonsinspeech.com/teaching-verb-tenses-with-tense-builder-app/` — index.
- **Does:** 48 short animated video lessons; child **watches a clip of an event**, then (Level 1) taps the matching picture/sentence, (Level 2) **drags the correct verb form** (3–7 choices) into the sentence; can **record their own voice** saying it. Strong on irregular past.
- **Best:** the event is *seen happening* before any form is chosen — progressive/past is a perception, not a label; drag + record = production. **Limit:** only past/present/future (simple) + irregulars; no perfect, no viewpoint system, no timeline. Copy the loop, not the scope.

#### 2.4 The rest of the interactive field — mostly naming, not manipulating
Jeopardy-style “Tense Time Machine” (`https://jeopardylabs.com/print/tense-time-machine-a1a2` — index), Flappy-style tense games (8-Bit Academy, `https://8bitacademy.com/games-by-skill/language-arts/grammar/` — index), and generic tense trainers all share one interaction: *see a sentence/clue → name or pick the tense*. None found (outside §2.1–2.3) lets the learner move an event, move a viewpoint, scrub time through an event, or toggle a result state. **That absence is the market gap.**

### 3. SLA research

#### 3.1 Aspect Hypothesis (Andersen & Shirai) — learners mark aspect before tense; sequence by verb type
Summary in Li & Shirai tradition, via Cantonese-learners chapter: `http://emktg.cengage.com/marketing/sample_chapters/2023/9789811653322_Samples.pdf` — index. Predictions, repeatedly supported:
1. Past/perfective marking appears **first on achievements & accomplishments** (telic: *broke, arrived, finished a cake*), then extends to activities, then states.
2. Progressive marking appears **first on activities** (*running, raining*), then extends to accomplishments, then achievements; it is (in L1) rarely overextended to states — but L2 intermediates *do* produce stative progressives (see §3.3).
3. Imperfective/background marking emerges later than perfective/foreground.
**Design consequence (strong):** introduce and practise each form with its *prototype verb class first* — simple past with telic events, progressive with activities — and only later deliberately stretch to non-prototypes (*I loved it* past-state; *I'm being silly*). A time machine that uses **one activity verb for all 12 cells** (paint/bake/eat) hides exactly the variable that drives acquisition, and will generate nonsense cells learners then imitate (perfect-progressive achievements, progressive states).

#### 3.2 Bardovi-Harlig — Discourse/Narrative Grounding Hypothesis
Learners use emerging morphology to distinguish **foreground** (main story line; clauses that move time forward; order can't be swapped) from **background** (scene-setting, elaboration, evaluation; doesn't advance the clock). Synthesis in the SLA-informed Catalan tense-aspect teaching design: `https://revistas.ucm.es/index.php/CLAC/es/article/download/76714/4564456558608/4564456624030` — index: “foreground marked with perfective morphology; background with emerging imperfective forms… **narrative texts are optimal** [for teaching] because prototypical associations between lexical aspect, discourse grounding and grammatical form coincide.”
**Design consequence:** the natural home of tense-aspect is a **story player**, not isolated sentences: foreground card → clock advances + simple past; background card → clock frozen + progressive/state; flashback card → past perfect. Reasoning F3.5 (§2.1) and Lingolia's comparison pages (§2.2) are the only learner materials found that teach this explicitly. Bardovi-Harlig's other robust finding — learners go **pragmatic (word order/context) → lexical (adverbials) → grammatical (morphology)** — explains Chinese learners' adverbial crutch (§3.3): instruction must eventually *remove* the adverbial scaffolding, or morphology never becomes the cue.

#### 3.3 Chinese learners' specific problems (corpus evidence)
Chinese has **no tense inflection**; time is carried by adverbials/context and aspect by particles **了 (le, perfective/change), 过 (guo, experiential), 着 (zhe, durative)** — none of which maps 1-to-1 onto English forms.
- **Overall tense misuse:** Duan (2011), CLEC/CET-4 writing — misuse concentrated in **simple present and simple past**, plus main/subordinate tense collocation in complex sentences; causes = interlingual + intralingual transfer, “vague understanding of the time concept indicated by different tenses” — `https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1081034` — index.
- **Present perfect — the epicentre.** CLEC conceptual-transfer thesis: learners treat the present perfect as “a simple description of the **complete event**”, and believe a temporal adverbial “showed a certain time orientation clearly and would not affect the verb aspect” — hence perfect with definite past adverbials; the stated root obstacle is that the perfect's **reference point is present** while the event is past — `https://globethesis.com/?t=2335330566959878` — index. Taiwan study (Ch.5): “**le and guo do not actually facilitate** learners' acquisition of the English present perfect because their uses are not totally parallel; learners have to undergo **restructuring a new concept of time and adoption of perspectives**”; heavy reliance on adverbials is partly L1-driven, and adverbial-association instruction is “inadequate” — `http://api.lib.ntnu.edu.tw:8080/server/api/core/bitstreams/6aaa4244-fde3-4014-924f-45c488ca1cde/content` — index. SWECCL/CLEC study: in CLEC, 1,078 present-perfect instances contained 91 (8.44%) form errors; Cai (2010) found learners translate 了-sentences as perfect *or* past — `https://www.sinoss.net/uploadfile/2013/0423/20130423093710258.pdf` — index.
- **Classroom-size replications (Hans-published, low-tier — direction only):** 83 Grade-8 pupils: tense confusion 39%, verb misuse 30%, **have gone / have been confusion 21%**, already/yet misuse 17% — `https://pdf.hanspub.org/ces_3096237.pdf`; high-school writing: tense confusion 42.3%, **time-adverbial/tense mismatch 31.7%**, subject-verb agreement 26%, with present-perfect omission cited at 68% (*I live here since 2020* for *I have lived…*) and the mechanism stated as “汉语通过‘了’‘过’‘已经’等词汇模糊表达时间” — `https://pdf.hanspub.org/ve_1721919.pdf` — index.
- **Oversupply as well as omission:** Mandarin ESL learners *oversupplied* present perfect, past perfect and past progressive on a fill-in task; “the presence of adverbials sometimes led them” to perfect choices; past-progressive oversupply was mainly on **atelic activities** (Aspect-Hypothesis-consistent) — `https://un-pub.eu/ojs/index.php/GJFLT/article/view/4849/version/3594` — index. Past perfect is repeatedly the hardest of the past trio for Chinese learners (Li & Choe 2017, summarised in search results at `https://www.earticle.net/Search/Result?sf=4&q=L2+errors` — index).
- **Progressive — underuse first, stative errors later, and natives aren't categorical:** IRAL (2021) learner-corpus study: L1-Chinese learners used progressive **significantly less** than L1-German learners, plausibly because Chinese progressive marking is *optional when context suffices*; intermediate/advanced learners of all L1s **do** use stative progressives; native speakers themselves used stative progressives at 8.36% (other native corpora up to ~20%) — `http://english.hnu.edu.cn/__local/C/05/04/D80C26C9EF8B1FAA6CD12932FA0_3042FFA5_1ADC97.pdf` — index. So “state verbs never take -ing” is a useful *starter* rule and a false *final* rule (cf. Azar's *being + adjective*, *I'm loving it*).
- **Past-tense omission / -ed & -s invisibility:** because Chinese carries time lexically, inflection is treated as decoration — learners drop -ed in narrative retell, mix present into past stories, and lose tense consistency across clauses (multiple Chinese studies above). Morphology must be made *load-bearing* in tasks (remove adverbials; shuffle word order) or it will be ignored.

#### 3.4 Does separating tense from aspect beat the 12-tense grid? — direct evidence (2026)
Machová et al., *Australian Journal of Applied Linguistics* 9 (2026), `http://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1511004.pdf` — index: an instructional presentation teaching **tense (present/past/future) and aspect (progressive, perfective/perfect) as distinct categories, combined compositionally one aspect at a time**, vs the traditional 12-tense model that “all” major coursebooks use. Pretest (4 weeks before) / posttest / delayed posttest (4 weeks after): the compositional model **benefited early EFL learners through reduced cognitive overload** (12 items → ~5 core concepts); **more advanced students already exposed to the 12-tense model improved less** — authors recommend an overall shift away from the 12-tense model, with a *gradual* transition for experienced learners. Caveat: single presentation intervention, Czech EFL context, “perfective” terminology in the paper conflates perfect with perfective — don't copy that label.
**This is the closest thing to a head-to-head verdict on the mission's exact question:** for *new* learners (Chinese primary/junior starters), don't enter through 12 cells; enter through 3 times + 2–3 viewpoints, compose later. For learners already drilled in 12/16, keep the grid visible as a translation map.

#### 3.5 Concept-Based Instruction / SCOBAs — materialised concept diagrams *work*
Gal'perin → Negueruela (2003, Spanish aspect): learners get a **SCOBA** — a materialised diagram/flowchart of the *whole concept* — then **materialised practice** with it, then **verbalisation** (explaining choices aloud *using the diagram*), then internalisation (diagram withdrawn). Negueruela's Spanish-aspect SCOBA moved learners from unsystematic rules-of-thumb to defining/using aspect without the chart (summary in CBI literature, § sources).
English tense-aspect replications:
- **Fazilatfar, Jabbari & Harsij (2017)**, Iranian middle school, **age 14, N=28 CBI vs 30 traditional, 8 weeks**: CBI treatment was Cognitive-Grammar-based; tense, lexical aspect and grammatical aspect were oriented *separately* through a SCOBA diagram (Fig.1) before being combined. Both groups improved; **CBI group performed significantly better** on concept definitions, written discourse performance, and grammatical questions — `https://ilt.atu.ac.ir/?_action=article&au=64455&_au=Rezvan++Harsij` — index.
- **Benhamlaoui (2021)**, Essex PhD thesis, N=45, CBI vs PPP (presentation-practice-production) for past simple / past continuous / present perfect: **CBI more effective** for both concept understanding and form use, on post + delayed tests; tellingly, learners *preferred* the traditional treatment despite learning less from it — `http://repository.essex.ac.uk/id/eprint/29952` — index.
**Design consequence:** a v2 app should ship a **one-screen concept map (SCOBA) that stays available during every task**, and tasks should sometimes require a *why-explanation* (tap the reason on the map: “R is at the station; the leaving is before R”) — verbalisation is an active ingredient, not decoration.

#### 3.6 The “12 tenses” critique & future as modality
- Morphologically English has **two tenses** (past vs non-past/present: *swam/swim, was/am*); *will* changes **mood/modality** (certainty, volition, prediction), not tense; periphrasis with *be/have* changes **aspect**. Clean learner-facing statements: `https://canyouenglishplease.com/there-is-no-future-tense-in-english/` and Academic Marker (tense = fixed position, aspect = periods, modality = possibilities; only past & present tenses) `https://academicmarker.com/grammar-practice/verb-functions/verb-tense/is-it-true-there-are-only-two-verb-tenses-in-english/` — index. A Thai study of the two-tense approach reports the practical payoff: learners stop memorising a formula per “tense” and can *see* that tense lives on the **first verb** of the phrase, with aspect/mood layered after it — via `https://www.readkong.com/page/two-tenses-an-alternative-to-teaching-english-grammar-7223205` — index.
- This does **not** mean telling a 10-year-old “there's no future tense.” It means the future column must be labelled/designed differently: **“Talking about the future”** — a choice among will (decision/prediction, with a certainty dial) / going to (plan/visible evidence) / present continuous (fixed arrangement) / simple present (timetable) — exactly as Reasoning F3.6 and Woodward already teach it. A symmetric “future × aspect” column falsely implies *will be painting* is as central, and formed the same way in meaning, as *was painting*.

### 4. Sequencing; the present perfect; progressive as “inside the event”

**Aspect-first or tense-first?** Neither, in the naive sense. The evidence supports **time-anchor first, viewpoint immediately after, form composed last**, sequenced by prototype:
1. **Anchor:** past vs now with telic, visible-result events (simple past prototype) — *broke, finished, arrived yesterday* (Aspect Hypothesis §3.1; timeline §1.1).
2. **Inside vs whole:** progressive with *activities* at a frozen moment vs simple with the whole event (camera zoom §1.3; TenseBuilder freeze §2.3).
3. **Look-back:** perfect *only* as E-before-R with a **visible result/state at R** (Murphy §1.4), first with R = now (present perfect), then with R moved into the past (past perfect = “the past of the past” becomes trivial once R moves) and into the future.
4. **Compose:** perfect-progressive last and rarely (it's the composition of look-back + inside), and future-perfect cells de-emphasised/greyed.
This is, in effect, Reasoning's own chapter order (forms → present contrasts → story pasts → futures) with the machine *revisited* at step 3–4 rather than front-loaded.

**Making “current relevance” manipulable (present perfect lab — best components found):**
- **Result-state photo toggle (Murphy):** event card *lost his key* → NOW photo: ✗ key in hand / empty pocket, can't enter. Flip the result off (“he found it”) and the perfect becomes infelicitous — the app should *ask* “So now… does he have it?” before asking “which tense?”
- **Unfinished-time window (Seonaid/EnglishPage):** a resizable window ending at NOW/R (*today / this week / my life*). Events inside the window can be perfect; drop a finished label (*yesterday, in 2020, last week*) and the window slams shut → only past simple survives. This single widget teaches the *have seen him yesterday* error better than any rule statement.
- **Experience counter (EnglishPage/Seonaid):** Xs accumulate inside the life-window with a counter (*been to Paris ×3, never…*); adding a specific date ejects the event from the perfect window into the story line (past simple + follow-up details — Murphy's “give news in perfect, continue in past” pattern).
- **been vs gone as a map with a return trip:** person icon travels to a place; **gone** = icon still there (result at now: *where is he?*); **been** = icon back home with a souvenir/experience stamp. Directly targets the 21% Chinese-learner confusion (§3.3).
- **for/since as drag-type, not vocabulary:** *since* accepts only a **point** dropped on the ruler (2019, Monday, age five); *for* accepts only a **stretch** dragged along it (two years, an hour). Reasoning's mini-game already states this rule; making the *objects* different shapes (pin vs rubber band) makes it physical.
- **just / already / yet** as positions relative to expectation at R (just = just before R; already = before expected; yet = expected-by-R, still not at R) — BBC/Murphy treatment, positionable on the window edge rather than memorised as “marker words”.

**Progressive as inside-the-event (manipulable media):**
- **Scrub & freeze:** an animated event bar (CSS/canvas loop, no video file needed) with a playhead; learner drags the playhead *inside* the bar → progressive becomes available/true; drag it outside (before start / after end) → it doesn't. Pause mid-action (TenseBuilder loop §2.3) and ask “are we inside or outside?”
- **Zoom/aperture switch:** same scene rendered wide (whole event incl. endpoints, simple) vs zoomed/cropped (endpoints out of frame, progressive) — Smith/Comrie §1.3. The crop *is* the explanation of why progressive doesn't assert completion (“was building a house” ≠ built it).
- **Interruption snap:** a background progressive bar running; a foreground point-event drops onto it (*was cooking **when** the lights went out*) — the canonical past-progressive use, and the bridge into the story player (§3.2).

### 5. Production practicalities — offline single-file HTML

All top-ranked models below are buildable in one HTML file with inline SVG/CSS/JS, no assets, no network:
- **Timeline + event bars:** SVG line, draggable `<rect>` bars (pointer events); playhead scrub = range input or pointer drag; animation via `requestAnimationFrame` / CSS animations with `animation-play-state` for freeze-inside.
- **E/R/S markers:** three draggable tokens on the same SVG (Me-Now fixed at right in “real” mode, unlockable in story mode); sentence rebuilds from relative order of E/R — the ordering logic *is* Reichenbach's table, ~13 orderings collapsing to the taught forms.
- **Zoom viewpoint:** CSS `clip-path`/scale on the scene container (wide ↔ crop), or an aperture overlay that hides event endpoints.
- **Result-at-R photo:** two-state illustration (SVG groups toggled) + a yes/no “so now…?” check.
- **Story player:** card sequence data array; foreground cards advance a clock variable, background cards don't; flashback cards move R back. Pure state machine.
- **Block builder:** Reasoning's recipe is a token chain where each token constrains the next token's form — a small array + morphology table (V1/V2/V3/-ing per verb, with irregular families) — already proven in the parent design pattern.
- **SCOBA overlay:** one inline SVG concept map, toggleable over any task.
- What *not* to build: real video (use 6–12-frame sprite/CSS loops), speech recognition, accounts, or a 16-cell Chinese-style grid with equal visual weight for vanishingly rare cells.

## (b) Verdict on the 3×4 form-grid time machine: **MODIFY — keep as map & engine, replace as centrepiece**

- **Keep:** (i) the *compositional insight* — time × aspect — which the 2026 head-to-head evidence supports over 12 memorised formulas (§3.4); (ii) the **block-chain recipe** (§2.1), the best form-builder found; (iii) the grid itself as an always-available **summary map** (Chinese school/exam culture teaches 12/16 grids — learners need to be able to *translate* their classroom grid, §2.5).
- **Modify:** (i) read the grid **by aspect rows** as well as time columns (Lingolia §2.2); (ii) **shade by frequency/use** — future perfect progressive etc. greyed/collapsed, core 6–8 cells foregrounded (Chinese curriculum itself stages ~5 primary + 3 junior tenses); (iii) relabel the future column **“Talking about the future”** with its 4-way meaning choice inside (§3.6); (iv) rotate the protagonist event across **verb classes** (state/activity/accomplishment/achievement), greying impossible/odd cells (*is knowing*, perfect-progressive achievements) instead of generating them as equals (§3.1).
- **Replace (the centrepiece interaction):** “pick a cell → see the sentence” becomes **“set the scene → the form follows”**: place the event bar → place Me-Now → place/move the Story-Camera (R) → choose the viewpoint (wide / inside / look-back) → *then* the machine builds the verb block-by-block and names the cell it landed in. The grid becomes the *output/scoreboard*, not the input. This preserves everything the current app (per the mission description: 3×4 machine + result-state switch + self-placed events) already does well — the result-state switch *is* Murphy's model (§1.4) and self-placed events are the seed of #1 — and fixes its structural blind spots: no R, no viewpoint, no verb-class, no discourse.

## (c) Concrete upgrade list for the verb app — prioritised by learning value for Chinese primary/junior learners

**P0 — do first (attack the documented Chinese-learner errors & the missing R)**
1. **Draggable Story-Camera (R) token** on the existing timeline, distinct from NOW and from the event. Default locked to NOW (present perfect/past simple contrasts); unlock in story mode. Sentence rebuilds live as R moves. *(Targets: past perfect = hardest form; perfect's “reference point is present” obstacle — §3.3, §1.2.)*
2. **Viewpoint switch as zoom, not buttons:** Wide / Inside / Look-back control that visually crops the event (endpoints in/out of frame) and shows/hides the result-at-R. Rename aspect labels for children: *Whole picture / Inside the action / Looking back at it*. Keep English terms as subtitles for exam alignment.
3. **Adverbial trap + “no-clue mode”:** (a) dropping *yesterday / in 2020 / last week* onto a present-perfect sentence visibly breaks it (window slams, §4); (b) a mode that **removes all time adverbials** so morphology is the only cue — directly countering the lexical-stage crutch (Bardovi-Harlig §3.2; Taiwan adverbial-reliance finding §3.3).
4. **“So now…?” result check before tense naming:** every perfect item first asks the Murphy question (photo/state at R: *does he have it now? is she still there?*), only then builds the form. Extend the app's existing result-state switch from present perfect to past perfect (result at *past R*: “when we arrived, the train had left → platform empty *then*”).
5. **Story player (minimum viable):** 6–8-card stories; learner assigns each card foreground/background/flashback by choosing its form; clock advances only on foreground cards. Include the interruption pattern (*was cooking when…*) as a template. *(§3.2; Reasoning F3.5 as content model.)*
6. **Verb-class gate + prototype sequencing:** tag every verb in the app state/activity/accomplishment/achievement (endpoint flag icon). **P1 — present-perfect lab & future lab (the two columns the grid teaches worst)**
7. **Unfinished-window widget** (§4): resize *today/this week/my life*; experience counter (Xs + ×N + never); specific-date drop ejects event to past-simple story line + follow-up sentence in past (news pattern).

## (d) Known traps — models that look rigorous but teach wrong things

1. **Grid symmetry = importance symmetry.** 12/16 equal cells imply *will have been painting* matters as much as *painted*; it is vanishingly rare, and Chinese 16-grids add a whole 过去将来 row used mainly in reported speech. Ungreyed, the grid wastes working memory — the exact cognitive-load effect measured in §3.4.
2. **“Perfect = completed/finished （完成）.”** The Chinese name 现在完成时 and icons like 英语兔's *all-carrots-eaten* teach completion; but perfect is **anteriority + relevance at R**, and its unfinished uses (*have lived here for years, has been painting*) contradict “finished”. This is the single most damaging trap for Chinese learners (§3.3).
3. **“Progressive = happening now.”** Fails for past/future progressive, temporary-around-now, background, and future arrangements; also generates the reverse error of using simple present for actions happening now (EnglishPage names this the most common learner mistake).
4. **Marker-word （标志词） teaching.** *yesterday→past, already→perfect, now→progressive* cue-spotting produces adverbial-dependent learners who produce **I have seen him yesterday* (two “clues” collide) and collapse when no clue is present (§3.2, §3.3). Marker words are training wheels that must be *removed by design*.
5. **Position-only timelines.** X-on-a-line cannot represent habit, states, unspecified time, or relevance; simple present drawn “at now” and perfect drawn “in the past” both teach falsehoods (§1.1). Every timeline needs a viewpoint/result channel.
6. **One-to-one L1 mapping:** 了 = past/perfect, 过 = present perfect (experience), 在/着 = progressive. Each is *sometimes* right, which makes the mapping stickier and wronger; corpus work shows le/guo do **not** facilitate present-perfect acquisition (§3.3). Contrast, don't equate: show the same 了-sentence requiring past in one context and perfect in another, decided by R/result.
7. **“Future = will + verb” as a tense column.** Hides going to / arrangements / timetables, hides will's modal meanings (promise, instant decision, certainty), and breaks after *when/if* (§3.6).
8. **Absolute stative ban.** “State verbs never take -ing” is falsified by native use (*I'm being careful, I'm loving it*, corpus rates §3.3); teach as “states default to simple; -ing changes the meaning to temporary behaviour” for junior learners, plain ban only as a primary-level starter — and label it as such.
9. **Reichenbach letters / SCOBA diagrams as worksheets.** E-R-S initials or a dense CBI chart, presented as a poster to memorise, lose the entire benefit: both work only when *manipulated/verbalised* (§3.5 — CBI learners even preferred the worse traditional method; engagement must be designed, not assumed).
10. **Gamified naming (Flappy/Jeopardy “time machines”).** Speed-naming a tense from a clue trains label recall, not the anchor/viewpoint decisions that constitute competence; keep such games as warm-ups only (§2.4).
11. **Perfective ≠ perfect.** Some academic sources (including the 2026 study in §3.4) call the have+V3 aspect “perfective”. In English pedagogy, *perfective* (whole-event view) aligns with the **simple** view, while **perfect** is the look-back/anterior view. Mixing the labels in a children's app will corrupt the viewpoint switch (#2 above).
