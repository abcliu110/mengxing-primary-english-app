# How the Best Products and Research Teach English Verbs — Tense/Aspect, Polysemy, Irregular Forms, Phrasal Verbs

## Summary

**No single product teaches a verb the way principled polysemy (Tyler & Evans 2003) teaches a preposition** — i.e. one spatial core sense, drawn as a schema, from which every other sense is shown to *extend*, so the learner can predict unseen senses. That treatment exists on the web for **particles** (Rudzka-Ostyn's *Word Power*, 2003) and, as pure analysis, for the **interaction of a verb network × a particle network** (Mahpeykar & Tyler 2015, on *get/take × up/out*), but it has never been turned into a learner-facing interface for an individual verb such as *get, take, make, run* or *hold*. Every commercial treatment of *get* found is a flat list grouped by construction (`get + noun = obtain`, `get + adjective = become`…), not a network.

What *does* exist, in pieces:

1. **For tense/aspect geometry, one genuinely manipulable exemplar:** Reasoning School's **"Tense Time Machine"** (F3 Verbs and Tenses) — verified live. The learner slides time (past/present/future) and switches aspect (simple/continuous/perfect/perfect-continuous) and watches the same event redraw on a timeline and rebuild its verb form block-by-block (`3 × 4 = 12`). The same page also contains the best pattern-grouped irregular-verb treatment found and a two-event story timeline for past perfect. It is the closest thing to a *prototype model for the tense half* of a verb.
2. **For phrasal-verb polysemy, the prototype model is a book, not software:** Rudzka-Ostyn (2003) *Word Power: Phrasal Verbs and Compounds* — chapters organised by particle, literal spatial sense first, then diagrammed metaphorical extensions, ~1,100 phrasal verbs across 17 particles. Its classroom replication results are mixed (see §5).
3. **For enactment, Cambridge's Phrasal Verbs Machine / Phrasalstein** is the strongest interface: dial a verb × a particle, watch a short animation of that exact combination. But it teaches each combination as a discrete item — the opposite of a network.
4. **For children, the strongest verb interface is a method, not an app:** TPR (Total Physical Response), plus song-and-act products (Lingokids). No publicly verifiable Chinese children's app (斑马 / 叽里呱啦 / 洪恩 / 伴鱼 / ABCmouse 中国版) exposes a verb-specific unit that could be examined; only system-level descriptions are public.
5. **Research evidence is sobering:** cognitive-grammar tense instruction is "at least moderately effective… comparable to traditional" (Bielak & Pawlak 2011, mostly *explicit* knowledge); simply handing learners a core-sense + peripheral-senses text for *hold/put/run* produced **no significant gain** (Akamatsu 2010); a Rudzka-Ostyn mind-map treatment **did not outperform** memorisation+translation on the post-test (Al-Otaibi 2019). The network idea is analytically strong and pedagogically unproven in its passive form — it needs to be *manipulated*, not just shown.

Confidence: high for the tense and phrasal-verb findings (multiple live/index sources agree); medium for children/Chinese apps (public evidence is thin — flagged in *Could not verify*).

## Findings

### 1. Tense/aspect visualization and interaction

#### 1.1 Reasoning School — F3 Verbs and Tenses / "The Tense Time Machine" — **verified live**
URL: https://www.reasoning.school/learn/english/foundation-03.html

What the learner sees and does:
- **F3.3 Tense Time Machine.** Premise stated on the page: every tense answers two separate questions — *When?* (past/present/future, 3 choices) and *How do we see the action?* (aspect: simple / continuous / perfect / perfect-continuous, 4 choices) — "3 × 4 = 12 tenses. That's all there is. Drive the time machine below: slide through time, switch the aspect, and watch Meera's rangoli on the timeline." A slider (`time: past, present, future`) plus four aspect buttons redraw the timeline picture and the sentence (*Meera paints a rangoli every Diwali* → other forms), with a "recipe card": time goes on the *first* helper; continuous adds *be + -ing*; perfect adds *have + V3*; perfect-continuous uses both.
- **"Snap a tense together, block by block"**: steps through *By 6 p.m., Meera will have been painting for three hours* — TIME: FUTURE (*will*) → PERFECT: HAVE → CONTINUOUS: BE→BEEN → MAIN VERB -ING — teaching that "every block decides the shape of the next."
- **F3.1 Verb Wardrobe.** Type any verb; it builds all five forms (V1, V1+s, V2, V3, -ing) in sentence frames ("Yesterday I ___", "I have ___", "I am ___") and names the spelling rule. It explicitly warns V3 and -ing "never stand alone" — directly targeting the bare-verb / missing-helper errors documented for Chinese learners (§5).
- **F3.5 Story timeline.** "Two past events on one timeline": *When Paati reached the station, the train had left* — simple past = the reference point that moves the story forward; past continuous = background already in progress; past perfect = a jump further back. This is aspect taught as *narrative geometry*, not as a list of uses.
- **Present perfect section (F3.4)** is headed "the past that still matters": *I have lost my key (I still can't get in)*; experiences, visible present results, *since* + point vs *for* + length, and the classic error *I have seen him yesterday → I saw him yesterday*.
- Games interleaved: Tense Detective, Tense Doctor ("each sentence has one sick verb — tap the word that's wrong"), Verb Bubble Pop, "Which family?" for irregulars (see §4).

Expresses well: **tense geometry and aspect contrast** (the only interface found where tense is a *machine the learner drives*, not a chart the learner reads); **form-building / production** (block assembly); participle system (V3's dependence on *have/be* is made structural).
Limitations: one anchor verb/scene per tense cell (painting a rangoli); no polysemy at all; English-medium for Indian NIOS learners — no L1-Chinese contrast; the timeline itself is pre-drawn per cell — the learner does not place *their own* events on it.

#### 1.2 Lingolia — Interactive English Tenses Timeline — **verified live**
URL: https://english.lingolia.com/en/grammar/tenses/timeline

What the learner sees and does: a grid — columns Past / Present / Future, rows Simple / Progressive / Perfect / Perfect-Progressive. "Click on a tense or an aspect to get started." Each cell shows the aspect's shape (X marks for simple events, bars for progressive, etc.) with example sentences. The aspect definitions are unusually good and *relational*: perfect = "you view a completed action as **connected to a later time**… the result of the action is emphasised… actions take place **earlier than a specific time**"; progressive = "ongoing, incomplete or temporary."
Expresses well: whole-system overview; perfect-as-relation-to-a-later-reference-point (the closest a mainstream site comes to Reichenbach-style E–R–S without naming it).
Limitations: click-to-reveal, not manipulable — you cannot drag an event, change its duration, or watch past simple morph into present perfect; no production; examples are generic.

#### 1.3 Perfect English Grammar (Seonaid) — Present Perfect — index
URL: https://www.perfect-english-grammar.com/present-perfect-use.html

What the learner sees and does: no timeline graphic at all — instead the cleanest *verbal* decomposition of present relevance found anywhere: **Unfinished actions** (started in past, continue to present; *since* + fixed point / *for* + period) vs **Finished actions** split into (2) life experience with a living subject (*ever/never*), (3) unfinished time word (*this month, today* — "NOT: *I've seen him yesterday*"), (4) **finished action with a result in the present** — *I've lost my keys (so I can't get into my house)*, (5) recent news (*just/yet/already*). Plus the *been* (visited and came back) vs *gone* (went and is still there) contrast — a result-state distinction most interfaces never make.
Expresses well: **perfect aspect's "present relevance"** — as a taxonomy. This is the content a spatial interface should encode.
Limitations: entirely static text + separate drill PDFs/exercises; the learner never *sees* unfinished time or a result-state; nothing connects this page's five uses into one core meaning ("anteriority + current relevance") — they are presented as five uses, i.e. a list.

#### 1.4 The static-timeline mainstream: English Page, English Lessons Brighton, Teacher Thriving — index
- **English Page Verb Tense Tutorial** (cited via Principia/Yale/Sheridan pages referencing http://www.englishpage.com/verbpage/verbtenseintro.html — note: that URL comes from citing pages, not from a live fetch of English Page itself): per-tense mini-diagrams — simple present as repeated X's along a line, simple past as a short completed action or a bounded extended period (*I lived in Mexico for six years*), present perfect as unspecified-time event / change over time. Historically influential; each tense gets its *own* little picture, but pictures are never superimposed for contrast, and exercises are fill-in + "Check" button.
- **English Lessons Brighton — "All the English tenses, timeline"**: https://englishlessonsbrighton.co.uk/all-english-tenses-timeline-infographic/ — all 12 tenses on *one* timeline infographic (from *The English Tenses* book). Present perfect glossed correctly as "completed in, or has relevance to, the present." A poster, not an interaction.
- **Teacher Thriving — Teaching Perfect Verb Tenses**: https://teacherthrive.com/teaching-perfect-verb-tenses/ — the canonical classroom X-on-a-line method: past perfect = two X's (earlier X = *had travelled*, later X = *moved to Spain*); present perfect = one event X plus an X *on the present point*; future perfect = two X's in the future. Useful precisely because it shows the *reference point* moving — but it is a teacher anchor-chart/mini-book, not software.

#### 1.5 Drill-template layer: Genially / Wordwall / Kahoot hubs — index
- Genially "VERB TENSES" hub: https://view.genially.com/636d60d8e6cd6a0018aa8566 — an index page linking Wordwall/Kahoot/Liveworksheets activities: drag-and-drop, "balloon train", escape rooms, classify — organised as *Present perfect / Past simple & continuous / Present perfect – regular v / – irregular v* levels LV1–LV8. A Genially drag-and-drop verb-tense activity (https://view.genially.com/696127556cf4b363421ef8b6/interactive-content-drag-and-drop-verb-tenses) has learners drag forms (*spoke, wrote, began…*) to match base verbs.
- Assessment: this layer gamifies *form recognition and tense labelling*. The drag target is a category box or a gap, **never a timeline the learner constructs**. No template found in Genially/Wordwall search results asks learners to place an event on a line and let the line *decide* the tense — the spatial reasoning is done by the teacher who authored the item, not by the learner.

#### 1.6 Chinese-language tense teaching — index
Chinese sources are dominated by the **16-tense table** (4 时 × 4 态) + formula + marker-word lists, e.g.:
- UTA summary: https://www.uta.edu.cn/wgyxy/2016/0911/c2995a123119/page.htm — "现在完成时是联系过去和现在的纽带" (the present perfect is the link/bond between past and present); past simple "与现在没有联系" (no connection to now). The *bridge* metaphor exists in Chinese pedagogy as a **sentence**, not as a drawable/manipulable bridge.
- FlexiLingo (Chinese edition): https://www.flexilingo.com/zh/blog/english-tenses-simplified — present perfect "把过去和现在连接起来——某事在之前发生了，而且现在仍然有影响"; contrast taught as 过去时 = 强调「何时」 vs 完成时 = 强调「经历/结果」; past perfect as 「倒叙」 (flashback) creating "一条清晰的时间线". Again: good metaphors, zero interaction.
- Hand-drawn timeline blog posts exist (e.g. cnblogs "一文搞懂英语主要时态（附全套手绘时间轴）", https://www.cnblogs.com/hoyong/articles/14281538.html) but are static images.
**Finding:** in the Chinese-language web searched, tense visualisation stops at tables, formulas, signal-word lists (ever/never/already/since/for…) and static hand-drawn axes. No Chinese product/page found offers a manipulable timeline.

### 2. Verb polysemy teaching — core sense → network

#### 2.1 The analytic gold standard that is *not* a product: Mahpeykar & Tyler (2015) — index
URL: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-and-cognition/article/principled-cognitive-linguistics-account-of-english-phrasal-verbs-with-up-and-out/BFC34113FA07418EFBAF90A997BE52A0 (PDF via Cambridge core mirror in Sources)
Claim: previous work analysed only the *particle's* polysemy; this corpus (COCA) study builds **polysemy networks for both the verb and the particle** — *get* and *take* (following Langacker 1991) × *up* and *out* (following Tyler & Evans 2003) — and shows the multiple meanings of *get up, take up, get out, take out* are **compositionally formed through the interaction of the two networks**. This is exactly the "verb treated the way cognitive linguistics treats a preposition" model the mission asks about. It exists as a journal article with network diagrams. **No learner product, app, or courseware found implements it.**

#### 2.2 The teachable prototype: Rudzka-Ostyn, *Word Power: Phrasal Verbs and Compounds* (2003) — index (via Al-Otaibi 2019)
Evidence URL: https://jle.hse.ru/article/download/8170/10667
- Described in Al-Otaibi (2019) as "the first textbook on teaching phrasal verbs using a cognitive approach": ~1,100 phrasal verbs/compounds, **17 particles, chapters divided by particle**, literal senses taught **before** extensions, "imagery using diagrams containing image schemas", conceptual metaphor (body/mind as containers: *stuck out her tongue* → *thought out a solution*).
- What the learner does: systematised explicit focus + "exetests" (gap-fill choosing the correct phrasal verb).
Expresses well: **polysemy extension for particles** — the only complete, ordered, diagrammed core→extension curriculum for any verb-related item.
Limitations: (a) it networks the *particle*, holding the verb as a variable — the mirror image of the mission's question; (b) print book for post-intermediate/advanced learners, not software, not for children; (c) effectiveness is contested — see §5 (Al-Otaibi's own mind-map implementation did not beat the control).

#### 2.3 Particle-meaning teaching in the wild (mainstream, simplified networks) — index
- **English Listening World — Phrasal Verbs Complete Guide**: https://english-listening-world.com/pages/phrasal-verbs-complete-guide — Stage 3 "Recognize Patterns": UP = completion (*eat up, use up*) / increase (*speed up, turn up*) / appearance (*show up, come up*); OUT = completion-discovery (*figure out, find out*) / distribution (*hand out*) / extinction (*burn out, run out*); OFF = separation / stopping / journey-start. Learners collect 10 verbs per particle and group them by meaning pattern, then use the pattern to *predict* an unfamiliar phrasal verb — the only mainstream page found that trains prediction, the actual payoff of a network.
- **18k English (Chinese/English bilingual)**: https://18kenglish.com/zh/english-phrasal-verb-particles-explained/ and system page https://18kenglish.com/vi/english-phrasal-verbs-learning-system/ — "the particle carries its own consistent core meaning": UP core = completion + increase (*finish up, use up, save up, speed up, show up, clean up* — "in every case UP is pushing the action toward a finished or elevated result"); OUT = exhaustion vs bringing-hidden-into-the-open (*run out* vs *find out / figure out*). This is principled polysemy translated into learner prose, in Chinese-accessible form — but still prose lists, no diagram the learner manipulates.
- **Woodward English** (search-index only; one direct fetch failed and was not retried): per-phrasal-verb pages such as GET OUT (1. leave a place, 2. become known, 3. escape, 4. remove something) and PUT OFF / PUT UP, each ending in a "Summary Chart", plus GET phrasal-verb lists at https://www.vocabulary.cl/Advanced/Phrasal_Verbs/GET.htm. Meanings are numbered, illustrated and exemplified — but presented as **co-listed senses of the combination**, with no arrow back to a core sense of *get* or of *out*. This is the dominant commercial pattern: *polysemy acknowledged, network withheld*.
- **VOA / Perfect English Grammar treatment of GET itself** (index): https://www.editorialwords.com/getting-to-know-the-verb-get-voa-learning-english-dec-01-2016/amp/ and the widely copied "Meanings of GET" page http://adislanguageschool.com/meanings-get-english/ — *get* organised **by construction**: `get + object = obtain/receive/buy`; `get + place = arrive`; `get + adjective = become`; plus fetch, contract an illness, causative, get-passive, and a phrasal-verb appendix. This is the best *flat* treatment: the pattern (the following word-class) does the disambiguating work a network would do. It never claims obtain/become/arrive are related, and a learner cannot use it to guess a new sense.

#### 2.4 The research-only verb networks: *hold/keep*, *hold/put/run* — index
- Csábi's lexical-network analysis of **hold and keep** (EURALEX 2002, via https://www.academia.edu/1601824/Polysemous_Words_Idioms_and_Conceptual_Metaphors_Cognitive_Linguistics_and_Lexicography): a verb *is* analysed as a network of minimally differing senses (Norvig & Lakoff's Lexical Network Theory) — explicitly arguing there is **no single abstract sense** covering *hold an umbrella / hold an opinion / the offer still holds / my brain can't hold…*, so the honest model is a *chained network*, not one core dot. Important nuance for any prototype: for verbs, "core meaning" may need to be a small cluster + typed links (extension / specialisation / metaphor), as in the EURALEX 2010 phrasal-entry study (https://euralex.org/elx_proceedings/Euralex2010/131_Euralex_2010_9_PERDEK_Getting%20through%20to%20phrasal%20verbs_A%20cognitive%20organization%20of%20phrasal%20verb%20entries%20in%20monolingu.pdf), which found pedagogical dictionaries (LDOCE5 etc.) organise senses by *frequency*, which "often precludes cognitive continuity", and proposed spatial cognitive networks in entries instead. No dictionary product has adopted it.
- Akamatsu (2010) tested exactly this for **hold, put, run** with Japanese EFL learners — see §5 for the null result.

#### 2.5 A dissenting voice on particle-grouping — index
Tuton's tutor guide (2026): https://tuton.io/blog/how-to-teach-phrasal-verbs-online — argues the opposite sequencing: "stop sequencing them by particle (*get up, get on, get off*) — that's how textbooks teach them, and that's why your students forget them"; teach by **frequency** (PHaVE List, Garnier & Schmitt 2015; 25 phrasal verbs ≈ a third of all occurrences, Gardner & Davies 2007) and by **episodic/semantic context**, because "a B1 student doesn't yet have the L2 intuition to extract those patterns from a list" even though "the cognitive linguistics research is unambiguous" that particles cluster (*up* completion, *out* distribution, *off* cessation). Report both: the field's practitioner literature and its cognitive literature currently recommend *opposite organisations* of the same material.

### 3. Action-verb learning for children — TPR, gesture/video, manipulation, AR

#### 3.1 TPR as specified by the British Council — index/open
URL: https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/professional-development/teachers/managing-lesson/total-physical-response-tpr
Teacher (as "parent") says *jump* / *look at the board* and demonstrates; children do the action, then say the word while doing it, then command each other; Simon Says as the control game. Explicitly listed as usable for action vocabulary (*smile, chop, wriggle*), for **tenses and continuous aspect** (morning-routine sequences), imperatives and storytelling. Stated disadvantages, from the source itself: embarrassment, "only really suitable for beginner levels", "you can't teach everything with it and if used a lot it would become repetitive."
Research layer (index): a 2024 SAGE Open study (https://sage.cnpereading.com/paragraph/download/?doi=10.1177/21582440241288924) found *personalised* TPR — children invent their own movements for target words and produce videos — beat textbook-dance-video TPR for recall, especially at higher levels, precisely because standard TPR "does not allow students any freedom to creatively execute an order". A 2026 Vietnamese quasi-experiment (Grade 5, N=30, 4 weeks) reported large TPR gains over textbook routines (post-test d≈1.90) — small, single-centre, treat as indicative. Lindstromberg & Boers' enactment work (via ResearchGate summary) extends this upward: acting out manner-of-movement verbs (charades) helped learners interpret *unseen metaphorical* uses — the one bridge found between enactment (§3) and polysemy extension (§2).

#### 3.2 Products where children act / sing / manipulate verbs — index
- **Lingokids**: https://lingokids.com/english-for-kids/songs/this-is-the-way-song-for-kids — "This is the Way": characters perform *run and jump, walk and sing, throw a ball, climb a wall* while children sing and are explicitly invited to perform the actions ("perfect… to perform the action words described in it, so the little ones can start associating these words with their meanings"); "Wheels on the Bus" is likewise billed as teaching action verbs (http://lingokids.com/blog/posts/new-interactive-games-for-kids-in-the-lingokids-app?p=64154). What the child does in-app beyond the song is tapping games; the *body* work happens off-screen, prompted by the song. No tense contrast — verbs are present-tense action labels.
- **British Council LearnEnglish Kids — Actions 1/2/3 + Actions flashcards**: https://learnenglishkids.britishcouncil.org/parents/resources?page=1 — "Play some word games to practise actions vocabulary" at Levels 1–3, printable action flashcards. Word-game matching, not enactment and not manipulation of an on-screen body.
- **Toca Boca / My PlayHome (via speech-pathology practice guide)**: https://www.chatterbots.com.au/the-importance-of-verbs-and-how-to-teach-them — a clinician recommends Toca Life and My PlayHome as the best apps *for targeting verbs* because the child **manipulates characters to do actions** (milk a cow, fly a plane, cut hair) while an adult narrates — i.e. the verb teaching is supplied by the co-playing adult; the apps themselves contain no verb curriculum, labels or tense. This is the "manipulate a world, verbs emerge" model, and its dependence on a mediating adult is its documented limitation.
- **Khan Academy Kids / ABCmouse**: no verb-specific unit could be publicly verified; generic round-ups merely assert such apps "provide engaging grammar exercises". Recorded as *could not verify*, not as evidence.
- **AR / body-tracking**: only research prototypes found — TeachAR (https://www.computer.org/csdl/proceedings-article/ismarw/2016/07836534/12OmNB9t6ld) teaches colours/shapes/spatial relations to 4–6-year-old non-natives via ARToolKit + Kinect speech input — notably **spatial relations (the preposition domain), not verbs**; Kinect embodied games in preschool research are psychomotor/computational-thinking, not English-verb products. **No commercial AR/body-tracking English-verb product was found.** The body-tracking verb game remains an open slot.

#### 3.3 Chinese children's apps — index, system-level only
- **斑马 (Zebra / 斑马AI学)**: product analysis https://www.woshipm.com/evaluating/3388172.html/comment-page-1 — lesson loop: 外教视频 (5 min) + 单词认读 (2 min) + 绘本故事 + 口语练习 + 单词拼写 + "Zebra Call" AI video-call; AI-adaptive push of weak-point exercises. Verbs enter as vocabulary inside topics; no verb/tense unit is publicly described.
- **叽里呱啦**: founded 2014, 0–8, graded song/main-course/phonics system, all cartoon-taught (same analysis; app description https://www.32r.com/app/14376.html stresses songs, situational speaking, interactive games, themed word banks).
- **洪恩英语**: https://www.32r.com/app/83091.html — "字母、单词、句型、对话、阅读四阶路径", synced to school textbooks.
**Verdict for §3.3:** publicly verifiable evidence supports only "video + word cards + follow-along speaking + mini-games" for all three. Whether any of them teaches a verb as an *action the child performs* (camera/body) or contrasts verb forms could **not** be verified from public pages — the apps are paywalled and their public descriptions are marketing-level.

### 4. Irregular forms — pattern families vs alphabetical lists

The consensus across independent sources is unusually strong: **group by sound/form pattern, chant the group, then space the repetition.**

- **Reasoning School F3.2 (verified live)**, https://www.reasoning.school/learn/english/foundation-03.html — families: *all three the same* (cut-cut-cut; put, hit, let, shut, hurt, cost, set); *V2=V3* (buy-bought-bought; bring, think, teach, catch, keep, sleep, make, say); **i–a–u dance** (sing-sang-sung; ring, drink, swim, begin, sink — "walk down the vowels like a staircase"); *V3 ends in -en* (write-wrote-written; eat, take, speak, break, choose, drive, hide); *V3 ends in -own* (fly-flew-flown; grow, know, throw, blow); *loners* (go-went-gone, be, do, come-came-come). Game: "Which family?" — say the three forms, pick the family. This is pattern-grouping *plus* retrieval practice in one page.
- **BBC Learning English, 6 Minute Vocabulary "Irregular verbs 2" (2015, index)**: groups by rhyme/pattern — speak-spoke-spoken; grow-grew-grown; begin-began-begun / sing-sang-sung; write-wrote-written / drive-drove-driven — quiz: "swim, swam, ___?" Top tip: "combine irregular verbs with the same pattern in a short rhyme or story."
- **Pattern charts (index)**: Virtuous English Academy's 144-verb list (https://virtuousenglishacademy.com/reference/irregular-verbs.pdf) is explicitly anti-alphabetical in method: "Work down one group at a time rather than alphabetically. The groups below share a sound, and a group said as a chant (bought, brought, caught, fought, sought, taught, thought) sticks in a way seven separate cards never will" — groups: A-A-A; -ought/-aught A-B-B; i-a-u "vowel ladder" A-B-C; regular-past/irregular-participle (showed/shown). Wilford's memory guide uses the same AAA/ABB/ABA/ABC code system with beat-chanting ("mark three steady beats and say sing, sang, sung").
- **For children**: EnglishForKidz puzzle flashcards (https://www.englishforkidz.com/2018/06/irregular-verbs-puzzle-fashcards.html?m=0) — 105 verbs as 3-piece puzzles (picture + infinitive/past/participle), with the explicit rationale: "learning irregular verbs in the alphabetical order is always very difficult to children because these forms are associated with nothing in their minds… group them by rhyme" — though the author argues story-embedded selection beats any decontextualised grouping.
- **Woodward English (index)**: 101 irregular past-tense flashcards/charts and 101 past-participle charts, 72-verb bookmarks, past-tense word search — comprehensive reference material, organised as charts/lists rather than as named families with a game mechanic.
- **Spaced repetition layer**: Anki/Quizlet-style SRS is the standard recommendation in every strategy round-up found (front: base form; back: V2, V3 + example sentence; SM-2-style scheduling). Note the division of labour the sources converge on: **pattern groups supply the encoding, SRS supplies the retention schedule, sentence production supplies the transfer** — no single product found does all three for irregulars except, partially, Reasoning School (families + games, but no SRS schedule) — and Duolingo-style apps do SRS-like recycling without ever showing the family (see criticism in §5).

### 5. Research evidence — and Chinese learners' verb errors

#### 5.1 Does schema/timeline/cognitive teaching beat drills?
- **Bielak & Pawlak (2011)**, *Studies in Second Language Learning and Teaching* 1(3):365–400 — https://repozytorium.amu.edu.pl/items/8314cb2e-4b49-4059-a158-c36ca4eb17ff/full — quasi-experiment, N=50, three groups: cognitive-grammar descriptions (image schemas; present tense as a situation *coextensive with speech time*, visualised via Niemeier's "keyhole" — speech time is so short you see the situation through a keyhole; hence present simple vs progressive for speech-time situations, interacting with stative/dynamic verbs) vs traditional pedagogical-grammar descriptions vs control. Result, in the authors' words: CG instruction was "**at least moderately effective in terms of fostering mostly explicit grammatical knowledge** and its effectiveness turned out to be **comparable to** that of teaching based on traditional descriptions." Secondary summaries (Hung et al. 2018, https://e-iji.net/dosyalar/iji_2018_3_23.pdf) report it more strongly ("cognitive group outperformed traditional in immediate and delayed post-tests") — **the primary abstract is the cautious one; use it.**
- **Radden & Dirven (2007) *Cognitive English Grammar*** (review: https://linguistlist.org/issues/19/1503/) supplies the underlying tense model used by this literature: tense/aspect in a time schema with speech time, reference time and speaker viewpoint — a textbook for advanced undergraduates, never converted into learner software.
- **Phrasal verbs, cognitive vs list**: Kövecses & Szabó (1996, Hungarian learners, conceptual-metaphor grouping) and Yasuda (2010) report benefits; **Al-Otaibi (2019)** (https://jle.hse.ru/article/download/8170/10667), testing Rudzka-Ostyn via student-built particle mind-maps vs memorise+translate, found the experimental group "**did not outperform the control group on the posttest**", attributing it to senses missing from Rudzka-Ostyn's model, the verb×particle interaction not being made explicit, and *up/out* needing more focus. A Vietnamese quasi-experiment (2026, index) reports the cognitive approach (prototypical + derivative meanings, image schemas, metaphorical extensions, main-verb grouping) "emerged as a **potential** alternative" — hedged language, small literature. An embodied-vs-cognitive-vs-translation study (N=80, 29 phrasal verbs, via ResearchGate) found cognitive **and** embodied groups both significantly beat L1-translation — suggesting enactment may be the active ingredient as much as the network diagram.
- **Polysemous verbs, the direct test — Akamatsu (2010)**: https://jacet-kansai.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/2010articlesp.68-79-1.pdf — Japanese EFL learners tested on **hold, put, run**; the CL group could read a text explaining each verb's core and peripheral senses *during the test*; controls could not. "**No statistically significant difference**… simply introducing the core and peripheral senses of polysemous verbs may not induce learning by itself." This is the single most important cautionary result for the mission's core question: a network *displayed* is not a network *learned* — restructuring existing L1-shaped vocabulary knowledge needs more than exposure to the diagram.
- **Preposition comparison point**: for prepositions, the same design family *does* show clearer wins (e.g. Hung et al. 2018: CL group beat picture+explanation group on both spatial and metaphorical post-tests for 10 prepositions; Tyler, Mueller & Ho 2011 for *to/for/at*). Verbs are harder than prepositions for this method — plausibly because a verb's senses are chained/event-structured (Csábi) rather than radiating from one spatial configuration.

#### 5.2 Chinese learners' documented verb errors (Chinese + English sources agree)
- **Scale**: Hanspub error-analysis of Grade-8 writing (https://pdf.hanspub.org/ve_1722467.pdf): verb misuse = **56.8% of all grammar errors**; past-tense omission + 3rd-person -s omission = 50.7%, plus tense confusion 17.0% → **tense-related errors 67.7%** of the total. High-school past-tense study (80 compositions, https://pdf.hanspub.org/ml_2916243.pdf): past-tense **omission 40%**, irregular past spelling errors 26.7%, tense-consistency loss 20%, present-perfect misuse 13.3%.
- **Hardest forms**: CLEC-based study of Chinese college learners (https://www.sciscanpub.com/index/journals/ainfo/alr/6603.html): past perfect, simple past and present perfect show the worst progress rates; in the simple-past↔present-perfect confusion pair, learners over-use present perfect where simple past is required (42 tokens), traced by the author to the time-frame of experiential 过 and completive 了 — when no time/place is stated, 过/了 look equivalent to the present perfect, and learners over-generalise that equivalence to the frequent Chinese pattern (past time/place) + verb + 过/了.
- **Mechanism — tenseless L1**: Yang & Huang (IRAL 2004, via ResearchGate): Chinese has no grammatical tense; temporality is carried by pragmatic/lexical devices and by aspect markers — perfective 了/过, imperfective 在/着 — which "do not locate events in time". Classroom instruction forces an early start to tense use, but L1 habits prolong reliance on adverbials as *tense substitutes*. Review literature (2026, https://ahr.ewapub.com/article/view/36507.pdf) summarises transfer of 了→past/perfect, 过→perfect/experiential, 着→progressive, with systematic errors: tense confusion, missing subject–verb agreement, misuse of non-finite verbs.
- **Newer production evidence**: Huang, Qian & Chen (IRAL 2025, via EBSCO): 22 Chinese adolescents, story-retelling + elicited production — irregular verbs marked **13% higher** than regular verbs (salience helps), **bare verbs used for past tense in 12%** of obligatory contexts, and Mandarin-style resultative verb compounds used to encode past in **10%** — transfer is not only omission, it imports Chinese word-formation.
- **Controlled-task evidence**: Li & Choe (2017, N=142, https://www.kci.go.kr/kciportal/landing/article.kci?arti_id=ART002215516): learners *knew the forms* but used them inappropriately — past perfect hardest at both levels; simple past over-used for past events with present influence (the exact present-perfect core); present perfect over-used **with definite past adverbials** (*I have seen him yesterday* pattern); decisions driven by L1 translation, adjacent-tense consistency and local adverbial cues rather than by meaning.
- **Interface implication (no product acts on it)**: every one of these error patterns is *diagnosable by an interface* — bare verb where -ed belongs, perfect + finished-time adverbial, 了/过/着 mapping shown explicitly — yet **no product or courseware found, in either language, is designed around the Chinese aspect-transfer profile**. Chinese domestic teaching instead drills the 16-tense table and signal-word lists, i.e. it teaches *around* the documented errors rather than *at* them. This is the largest China-specific gap.

#### 5.3 Criticised / ineffective approaches (with sources)
- **Alphabetical / list memorisation of irregulars** — criticised by EnglishForKidz ("associated with nothing in their minds") and implicitly by every pattern-chart source in §4.
- **Phrasal verbs as arbitrary list + gap-fill** — criticised in Al-Otaibi's literature review (Moon 1997: presented "as arbitrary combinations which cannot be analyzed and rationalized"; textbooks suggest they "should be learnt mechanically") and by Tuton (list approach: "within a week, they'll remember three") — while Tuton *also* criticises the particle-sequenced alternative (§2.5). Both organisations are criticised; frequency + context is Tuton's proposed third way.
- **Duolingo-style drill as a verb system** — Ginseng English review (https://ginsengenglish.com/blog?offset=1495221136631&category=Grammar): method is grammar-translation, decontextualised sentences, no interaction; PsyPost analysis: "audio-lingual drilling… drilled again and again in decontextualised, effectively meaningless sentences", useful supplement for vocabulary/habit, "not a substitute". Its spaced recycling is real, but verbs are encountered as items inside sentences, never as a system (no timeline, no family, no network is ever shown to the learner).
- **Core-sense handout alone** — Akamatsu 2010 null result (§5.1).
- **Static timeline posters** — by implication of §1.5/§1.6: ubiquitous, but no study found showing a poster (as opposed to schema-based *instruction with tasks*) changes production; Bielak & Pawlak's gains were for explicit knowledge, "comparable" to traditional teaching.

## (b) Pattern library — reusable techniques mapped to the verb aspect each serves

| Technique | How it works (exemplar) | Verb aspect served |
|---|---|---|
| **Time × Aspect machine** | Two independent controls (3 times × 4 aspects); changing one redraws picture + rebuilds form (Reasoning School) | Tense geometry; aspect contrast; form-building |
| **Block assembly of the verb phrase** | will → have → been → -ing; "every block decides the shape of the next" (Reasoning School) | Participle system; production; missing-helper errors |
| **Verb wardrobe / five-form generator** | Type a verb, get V1/V1+s/V2/V3/-ing inside sentence frames + named spelling rule | Form patterns; participle awareness |
| **Two-event story timeline** | Reference event (simple past) vs earlier event (past perfect) vs background (continuous) on one line | Aspect contrast in narrative; past perfect |
| **X vs bar vs arrow notation** | Point events = X, ongoing = wavy/bar, perfect = arrow linking earlier event to a reference point (Teacher Thriving; Lingolia; English Page) | Tense geometry; perfect-as-relation |
| **Unfinished-time frame** | Today/this week/life-so-far drawn as an *open box containing now*; finished time words expelled from it (Perfect English Grammar, verbally) | Present perfect vs past simple; Chinese 过/了 over-generalisation |
| **Result-state pair** | Same past event, picture the *now* state: lost keys → can't get in; *been* (back) vs *gone* (still there) | Perfect aspect's present relevance |
| **Particle core → extension fan** | Literal spatial sense first, then grouped metaphorical extensions with image-schema diagrams (Rudzka-Ostyn; ELW; 18k) | Phrasal-verb polysemy; prediction of new items |
| **Verb × particle dial/matrix** | Spin verb wheel × particle wheel, enact the combination; reverse mode: enactment → name it (Phrasal Verbs Machine/Phrasalstein) | Phrasal-verb formation; form–meaning pairing |

| **Pattern families + chant** | i-a-u staircase, -ought rhyme family, -en family, AAA/ABB/ABC codes; say the family as one chant (Reasoning School; BBC; Virtuous) | Irregular forms |
| **SRS over families, not items** | Anki/Quizlet scheduling; card = whole family triplet + one sentence | Irregular-form retention |
| **Enactment / TPR + personalised movement** | Hear verb → do it → say it while doing it → command others; stronger when learners invent the movement and film it | Action-verb meaning (children); manner-verb metaphor transfer |

| **Song-with-obligatory-action** | Lyric *is* the action; singing requires performing it (Lingokids "This is the Way") | Action verbs, young children |

## (c) Gaps — aspects of verbs NO product expresses well

1. **Tense as a manipulable timeline — mostly gap.** Only Reasoning School lets the learner *drive* time/aspect, and even there the timeline is pre-drawn per cell. **No product found** lets a learner place/drag *their own* events, stretch an event's duration, move the reference point, and watch the correct tense *fall out* of the geometry. That interaction — the timeline as input, not illustration — does not exist on the public web in either language.
2. **Perfect aspect's "present relevance" — content exists, geometry doesn't.** Perfect English Grammar *says* it perfectly (unfinished time, present result, been/gone); nobody *shows* it as a manipulable state (e.g. toggle "result still holds now?" and watch perfect↔past-simple flip). Chinese pedagogy's bridge metaphor (联系过去和现在的纽带) is never drawn as a bridge the learner can break/repair.
3. **Polysemy networks for individual verbs — total product gap.** No app/courseware teaches *get* (or *take/make/run/hold*) as core/cluster → typed extension links → prediction of new senses. Analysis exists (Mahpeykar & Tyler; Csábi); flat construction lists are the commercial ceiling. This is the single clearest unoccupied slot.
4. **Verb × particle composition — unexploited.** The one analysis showing phrasal-verb senses *compose* from two networks (Mahpeykar & Tyler) has no interface; the one interface with a verb×particle matrix (Cambridge Machine) has no networks behind the dial. Putting the network *behind* the dial is an obvious, unbuilt combination.
5. **Participle system as a system — partial.** Reasoning School's wardrobe/block-assembly is the exception. Elsewhere V3 is a third column in a table, severed from its jobs (perfect, passive, *been/gone* result-states), which is plausibly why Chinese learners omit auxiliaries and misuse participles.
6. **Chinese aspect transfer — total gap.** The error profile is precisely documented (bare verbs 12%, perfect+finished-adverbial, 了/过→perfect over-extension, 着→progressive, RVC importation) and *no* interface in either language is built around it: no 了/过/着 ↔ English mapping visualiser, no "finished-time detector" game, no contrastive timeline starting from the Chinese sentence.
7. **Children: body as input — gap.** TPR is teacher-led; songs prompt off-screen movement; AR/Kinect verb games are research prototypes for other domains. No commercial product reads the child's body to teach/assess a verb, and no children's product progresses from action verbs to *verb forms* (the -ed / -ing system) — the child market and the tense market never meet.
8. **Networks that are *used*, not viewed.** Akamatsu's null result + Al-Otaibi's non-result converge: displaying a network/handout does not restructure knowledge. No product makes the learner *operate* a polysemy network (extend it, place a new sense on it, predict with it) — the interaction pattern that might fix the null results has not been tried in a shipped product.

## (d) Direct answer

**Does the web contain a verb-teaching approach strong enough to serve as a prototype model, comparable to what principled polysemy is for prepositions?**

**Not for verbs as a whole — but it contains two half-prototypes that, combined, specify one:**

- **For the particle half of phrasal verbs, yes:** Rudzka-Ostyn's *Word Power* (2003) *is* the principled-polysemy treatment applied to verb particles — core spatial sense, diagrammed extensions, prediction of new combinations — and Tyler & Evans' model is its direct parent. If the question is "has anyone done for any verb-related item what Tyler & Evans did for *over*?", the answer is Rudzka-Ostyn, in print.
- **For tense/aspect, the functional prototype is Reasoning School's Tense Time Machine** — not cognitive-linguistics-branded, but it embodies the same design philosophy: a small generative system (time × aspect, form-blocks) from which all 12 forms *derive*, manipulated by the learner rather than memorised as a list. It is the strongest *interface* exemplar found, verified live.
- **For a verb itself (*get, hold, run*…), no.** The correct analysis exists only in journals (Mahpeykar & Tyler 2015 for *get/take*; Csábi for *hold/keep*), the best commercial treatments are construction-frame lists, and the one controlled test of handing learners a core-sense text for *hold/put/run* found no effect. A prototype model for verb polysemy would have to be *built* — most plausibly as: Csábi/Mahpeykar-style chained network (not a single core dot) × Cambridge-Machine-style combinatorial manipulation × Reasoning-School-style "drive it yourself" interaction, with prediction tasks (place/guess a *new* sense) as the core mechanic, because the evidence says passive display fails.

In one line: **steal the tense machine from Reasoning School and the network logic from Rudzka-Ostyn / Mahpeykar & Tyler; the verb-polysemy product itself does not yet exist to be copied.**
