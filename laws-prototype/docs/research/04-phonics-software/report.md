# Phonics (自然拼读) in the World's Best Software Products & Research-Backed Programs

*Teaching sequence, interaction design, and practice/assessment loops — for children learning to read English, including ESL/EFL learners. Researched 2026-11-01. Flags: **verified live** = opened and read in the research browser this run; **index** = read via web-index search/fetch this run.*

## Summary

**The consensus core is unusually stable.** Every credible programme — Jolly Phonics, Read Write Inc. (RWI), Letters and Sounds / Little Wandle, Sounds-Write, UFLI Foundations, Teach Your Monster to Read — teaches grapheme–phoneme correspondences (GPCs) in an order chosen to **maximise early word-building, not alphabetical or phonetic-taxonomy order**: a first handful of high-utility letters (always some subset/order of *s a t p i n m d*) yields dozens of CVC words within days; visually/auditorily confusable pairs are separated; digraphs follow single letters; alternative spellings for a sound come before alternative pronunciations of a spelling. The research base supports *systematic* phonics strongly (National Reading Panel mean ES ≈ 0.44; EEF +5 months) but does **not** crown one brand, one sequence, or the multisensory extras: the NRP found no significant difference between phonics types, and the Orton-Gillingham meta-analysis found its branded interventions non-significant once pooled.

**Where products actually differ — and where design quality lives — is in five places:**
1. **Direction of organisation.** Print-to-sound programmes (Jolly, RWI, Letters and Sounds) browse *by grapheme*; speech-to-print / linguistic programmes (Sounds-Write Extended Code, Phonics International's Alphabetic Code Chart, sound walls) browse *by phoneme*, listing every spelling of a sound. The second model is the only one that natively handles variant pronunciations and complete inventories.
2. **How blending is embodied.** Best-in-class interactions: Alphablocks characters physically joining hands/fusing (blending and digraphs made visible); UFLI's word-chain building (change one grapheme, re-blend); RWI's Fred Talk → "Fred in Your Head" → speedy reading fade.
3. **Gating.** A spectrum: Lexia Core5 (adaptive branching + embedded assessment, the strongest assessment loop in the field) → UFLI/Little Wandle (mastery-paced classroom sequence with daily/weekly/termly review) → Hooked on Phonics (locked path + free-browsable library hybrid) → Reading Eggs (map + quiz gates) → Alphablocks/Khan Kids (pure free browse, no assessment). No consumer product does both strict mastery gating *and* open pattern browsing well.
4. **Irregular words.** The field has moved from whole-word memorisation (RWI "Red Words… just learn off by heart", classic Dolch flashcards) to **orthographic mapping with "heart words"** (UFLI, Really Great Reading): map every phoneme to its grapheme, mark only the 1–2 irregular graphemes "by heart", and distinguish *temporarily* irregular (not yet taught) from *permanently* irregular.
5. **Completeness.** Almost no product shows the learner *all* the words a pattern covers. Word lists are curated samples (RWI "Green Words", Jolly word banks, UFLI word chains). The honourable exceptions are reference artefacts, not learning loops: Phonics International's Alphabetic Code Chart / Phonics Spelling Dictionary (every grapheme per phoneme, with key words) and IXL's exhaustive skill index. **This is the single biggest open design space — and it is exactly the space the commissioning question asks about.**

**Chinese-market products** （斑马， 叽里呱啦， 洪恩， 腾讯ABCmouse, 伴鱼） invert the L1 order in one crucial respect and mostly get it right in marketing structure if not in depth: ABCmouse.cn explicitly sequences listening → speaking → phonics → writing, and 叽里呱啦 wraps each rule in a meaning-bearing animation before drilling it. But their phonics sequences are typically organised by *vowel taxonomy* (short vowels → long vowels → digraphs → blends) rather than early-word-building utility, default to **American pronunciation**, lean heavily on songs/chants as the memory mechanism, and publish no efficacy evidence. The standing Chinese criticism — phonics presupposes 1,000–5,000 words of *hearing* vocabulary that EFL children don't yet have, so "先记规则后套词" (memorise rules first, apply to words later) produces children who can chant rules but cannot read — is well-founded and is the key EFL design constraint.

**Direct answer (detail in §10):** No single product is the right model. The best blend for a *word-bank-complete, British-pronunciation* phonics module is: **Sounds-Write / Phonics International for the inventory architecture** (phoneme-organised, both directions of the code, variants as first-class objects) + **UFLI for the lesson/practice loop** (blending board, word-work word chains, heart-word routine, free) + **Teach Your Monster to Read for the child-facing game shell and British voice** (verified live) + **Lexia's branching/embedded-assessment pattern for gating** — with Alphablocks' fuse/hold-hands visual language for blending and digraphs.

#### 1.1 The GPC orders, compared

**Rationale, generalised.** (i) *Utility first*: s-a-t-p-i-n-m-d in any near-order generates sat, pin, tap, mad, sip… — the child reads real words in week 1, which is the motivational and orthographic-mapping engine. (ii) *Separate confusables*: b/d, p/b, m/n are spaced (Jolly puts p in Group 1, b in Group 3). (iii) *One sound per grapheme first* (the "simple code"), then *many spellings per sound* (complex code), then *many sounds per spelling* — Sounds-Write states this as four explicit concepts children are taught verbatim: letters represent sounds; a sound can be spelled with 1–4 letters; the same sound can be spelled different ways; the same spelling can represent different sounds (index, https://highcliffeprimary.org/reading/). (iv) Letters and Sounds Phase 4 and Sounds-Write Units 8–10 both insert a **structure-only plateau** (adjacent consonants, no new GPCs) — sequencing *word shape* separately from *code* is a reusable idea most apps miss.

**DfE validation.** After 3 evaluation rounds, **45 SSP programmes were validated** against 16 core criteria; there is no statutory requirement to use one, but validation = publisher self-assessment + expert panel judgement that all criteria are met (index, https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/choosing-a-phonics-teaching-programme/list-of-phonics-teaching-programmes). Jolly, RWI, Little Wandle, Bug Club, Phonics International, Sounds-Write, Monster Phonics etc. are all on the list — i.e. validation certifies *completeness/fidelity of the SSP model*, not comparative effectiveness.

#### 1.2 Programme-by-programme: what the learner sees and does

**Jolly Phonics (UK; app: Jolly Phonics Lessons / Jolly Phonics – Learn to Read).**
*Sees/does:* For each of the 42 sounds: a story, a physical **action** (e.g. weave like a snake for /s/), a **song**, the letter(s), animated letter formation to trace. The app's library is explicitly three routes — **Letter Sounds / Alternatives / Tricky Words** — with per-sound audio, all Jolly songs, animated formation, action image + instructions, a word bank and flashcards, games, and assessment tests whose results are shown to the adult, not as failure to the child. *Does best:* mnemonic redundancy for 3–5-year-olds — sound, story, action, song and formation all point at one GPC; the "Alternatives" route is an early, honest treatment of one-letter-many-sounds. *Limitations:* not a complete literacy programme (no vocabulary/comprehension strand); free app tier is a preview (one group unlocked); reviewers report buggy/degraded audio in the current edition — fatal in a phonics app; the action/song layer has no isolated evidence of added value (see §4), and Little Wandle deliberately omits it (below). Sources: index, https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.gilbertjolly.teachphonics.teachers&hl=en_CA ; index, https://www.phonics.org/literacy-review-jolly-phonics-program/ ; index, https://apps.apple.com/gb/app/jolly-phonics-learn-to-read/id1149029299

**Read Write Inc. (Ruth Miskin / OUP; primarily a taught programme, software = Oxford Owl home videos + school digital resources, no strong standalone child app).**
*Sees/does:* "Speed Sounds" lessons with picture-mnemonic cards ("Maisie, mountain, mountain" for m). **Fred Talk**: a frog puppet who can *only* speak in pure sounds — the teacher/Fred says "m-a-t", children blend "mat". Children pinch **Fred Fingers** (one finger = one phoneme) to segment for spelling. Word reading follows the mantra **"Special Friends, Fred Talk, Read the Word"** (spot the digraph first, sound out, blend) → fades to **"Fred in Your Head"** (silent blending) → **"Speedy Reading"** (at a glance). Every new sound arrives with its list of **Green Words** (fully decodable with the code so far); irregulars are **Red Words**. Lessons are tightly gated by half-termly assessment and group placement. *Does best:* the clearest *scaffold-fading* design in phonics (overt blending → covert → automatic) and the purest "pure sounds" discipline (no schwa "uh"). *Limitations:* its Red Word treatment in parent materials is literally whole-word ("words that don't work… just have to learn these words off by heart" — index, https://downsbrookprimaryschool.greenhousecms.co.uk/docs/Phonics_and_Reading_Guide_for_Parents_/A_Parents_Guide_to_Phonics_and_Reading_.pdf), which the heart-word model (§3) supersedes; the system lives in teacher delivery and print, so there is little child-autonomous software to copy.

**Letters and Sounds → Little Wandle Letters and Sounds Revised (DfE-validated; Collins Big Cat decodables).**
*Sees/does:* Four new GPCs Mon–Thu, **Friday review lesson**; every GPC practised in words, sentences, then fully decodable books matched to *secure* (not just taught) knowledge; the same book is read **three times with three different foci — decoding, prosody, comprehension**. GPCs and words are reviewed "daily, weekly and across terms" explicitly to move knowledge into long-term memory. Each grapheme has a *pronunciation phrase/mnemonic* for consistency — and, strikingly, **"Actions and songs are not used to avoid cognitive overload"** (index, https://www.sacredhearthillingdon.co.uk/curriculum/english/phonics-and-early-reading-little-wandle/). Parent videos model pure sounds for each phase (t not "tuh"). *Does best:* review cadence as a designed system (the closest classroom analogue to spaced repetition) and assessment-matched decodables. *Limitations:* a school programme, not software; its parent-facing digital layer is videos + PDFs.

**Sounds-Write (linguistic phonics, DfE-validated; software = practitioner training + decodable Dandelion Readers; also a free parents' course/app-style lessons).**
*Sees/does:* Lessons built from three skills only — blending, segmenting, **phoneme manipulation ("sound swap")**: build *sat*, swap one sound → *pat* → *pit*…, including with pseudowords once the code is secure. Word building is dictation-driven: hear the word, segment it, choose spellings, write it. In the Extended Code the unit of study flips: the class studies **one phoneme and all its spellings** (e.g. /ee/: e, y, ee, ea, ie). *Does best:* conceptual honesty about the full code (~175 correspondences for 44 sounds) and the phoneme-first organisation that a complete word-bank product needs; alternative pronunciations get their own units (ea = head/steam/steak). *Limitations:* austere — no characters, songs or game world; progression assumes a trained adult; little child-facing software polish to imitate.

**UFLI Foundations (University of Florida Literacy Institute; free Toolbox + free web apps).**
*Sees/does:* Every lesson, two days, eight steps: 1 Phonemic Awareness → 2 **Visual Drill** (flash grapheme, say sound) → 3 **Auditory Drill** (hear sound, write grapheme) → 4 **Blending Drill** → 5 New Concept → 6 **Word Work** → 7 Irregular Words → 8 Connected Text (decodable passage). The two free digital tools are the most copyable interactions in this entire survey: the **Virtual Blending Board** (teacher projects it, builds words from the lesson's word chain by swapping grapheme tiles; children read each word — decoding automaticity through minimal changes) and the **Virtual Word Work Mats** — Beginner (through Lesson 43) and Intermediate (digraphs, Lesson 44+) — where learners drag grapheme tiles to *encode and decode* word chains, on a projected board or individual devices. The Toolbox also gives away sound-wall cards, grapheme cards, heart-word cards, decodable passages, "Roll and Read" fluency sheets and home practice for all 128 lessons. *Does best:* the complete, explicit, *free* practice loop — teach → blend → build → read connected text — with encoding treated as equal to decoding. *Limitations:* US pronunciation; teacher-led by design (the blending board is explicitly a teacher projection tool); visuals are functional, not delightful; no adaptive engine. Sources: index, https://ufli.education.ufl.edu/foundations/toolbox-aus/apps2/ ; index, https://ufli.education.ufl.edu/foundations/toolbox/ ; index, https://www.slpliteracycorner.ca/blog/ufli-foundations-toolbox-for-literacy

**Teach Your Monster to Read (Usborne Foundation; Roehampton-designed) — verified live.**
*Sees/does:* The child creates and customises a monster and plays three sequential adventure games (~15 hours). **First Steps** (8 islands): hear a phoneme, tap/jump on the matching grapheme (e.g. *Flower Jump*: rescue a villager by landing on the correct grapheme; *Spaceship Builder*: fly over blocks to hear their sounds, choose the match); blending/segmenting mini-games with CVC words; "Trickies" are collectible characters — tickle one three times to collect it. **Fun With Words** (7 villages): new GPCs, CVC→CVCC/CCVC blending, sentences ("Get the cat" → "Can you get me an owl that is not green or red?"), *Hungry Beast* tricky-word feeding game. **Champion Reader**: alternative spellings and pronunciations, reading little books for meaning. The engine is **adaptive in a narrow sense: "Graphemes that a child is struggling with will be repeated"**; a separate **Practice Mode** lets a child/teacher target specific letter-sounds outside the adventure; a teacher dashboard shows strongest/weakest graphemes per child. *Does best:* motivation architecture (one long quest, collectibles, customisation) wrapped around a legitimate Letters-and-Sounds progression, in **British voices**, free on desktop. *Limitations:* assessment is repetition-based, not diagnostic (no branching re-teach); tricky words are taught as whole-word characters to collect — pre-heart-word design; an independent phonics.org review notes it occasionally introduces words using sounds not yet taught, and its /r/ and other articulations are specifically British (a feature here, a limitation in the US). Sources: **verified live**, https://www.teachyourmonster.org/teach-your-monster-to-read-overview/ ; index, https://www.teachyourmonster.org/teach-your-monster-to-read-mini-games/ ; index, https://www.phonics.org/teach-your-monster-to-read-review/

**Lexia Core5 (Lexia Learning / Rosetta Stone; PreK–Grade 5).**
*Sees/does:* After **Auto Placement**, the child works in themed levels (21 levels in current documentation; earlier editions 18 levels / 89 activities), five activities per level spanning phonological awareness, phonics, structural analysis, fluency/automaticity, vocabulary, comprehension. The signature loop is per-unit **three-step branching**: a *Practice* step → the *Standard* step; on error the child drops to an *Instruction* step that explicitly re-teaches and scaffolds the exact skill, then returns to Standard. Repeated branching turns an on-screen apple red, flags the child as "struggling" in **myLexia**, and prescribes a scripted teacher-led **Lexia Lesson**; completing a level generates a paper **Skill Builder**. All of this assessment is invisible — **"Assessment Without Testing®"** collects performance data without stopping for a test, and myLexia predicts end-of-year benchmark risk and prescribes usage minutes. *Does best:* the assessment/practice loop, full stop — it is the only product here where failure automatically produces (a) in-app re-teaching, (b) a human-teacher alert with a ready lesson, and (c) offline practice. *Limitations:* American pronunciation/content; the child experience is drill-like compared with Teach Your Monster; school-licensed and expensive; phonics is one strand of six, so pattern browsing by GPC/phoneme is not exposed to the learner at all. Evidence in §4. Sources: index, https://www.lexialearning.com/user_area/content_media/processed/Core5EfficacyResearch1.pdf ; index, https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/projects-and-evaluation/promising-programmes/lexia-reading-core5

**Reading Eggs + Fast Phonics (Blake/3P Learning, Australia).**
*Sees/does:* Core programme: a winding **map** of 120 lessons (12 maps × 10) after a placement test; each lesson 6–11 activities (letter of the lesson, songs, onset-rime and word-family games, segmenting/blending, sight words, a book); an **end-of-map quiz gates the next map**; rewards (eggs, avatar, games) throughout. **Fast Phonics** is the purer product: a Yeti world of 20 **"Peaks"** (levels), up to 24 parts each, sequenced to Letters and Sounds phases — systematic synthetic phonics without the core programme's analytic admixture. A separate **Spelling Skills Bank** (96 lessons) works word families/segmentation. *Does best:* sheer volume, polish and reward density for home users; the explicit two-track design (broad literacy map vs phonics-only Fast Phonics) is an honest admission that one sequence can't serve both. *Limitations:* the core programme mixes synthetic blending with onset-rime/word-family analogy and whole-word sight-word work, so its "systematic synthetic" claim fits Fast Phonics better than the flagship; lesson pace/accuracy criticised in independent review; evidence base is thin (see §4). Sources: index, https://readingeggs.com.au/articles/reading-eggs-or-fast-phonics-which-reading-program-to-use-for-students/ ; index, https://readingeggs.co.uk/homeschool/curriculum/

**Hooked on Phonics (US; app + monthly print Practice Packs).**
*Sees/does:* **Learn → Practice → Read → Celebrate**, repeated. Pre-Reader world (letters/sounds through songs and games) → Learn to Read world: each mini-lesson = a short video introducing a sound or pattern (e.g. -at, -an, sh) → reinforcement games (construct words, match words to sounds) → **a decodable book using only the sounds taught so far, after every lesson**, with optional audio + highlighting; at home, a workbook + 2–3 printed books + star stickers arrive monthly. Gating is **hybrid**: the learning path is sequential ("must complete each level before they can unlock the next") **but** songs, games and books are also freely accessible in a separate section. *Does best:* the tightest lesson→book coupling in consumer software and the digital+physical blend. *Limitations:* US; Common Sense Media notes repetition of activity types can feel tedious and error feedback is slow; decoding-only focus (supplement for comprehension); independent reviewers warn children can tap through without mastery unless an adult enforces accuracy. Sources: index, https://www.hookedonphonics.com/access-resources/faqs/ ; index, https://www.commonsensemedia.org/app-reviews/hooked-on-phonics-learn-read

**Khan Academy Kids (US, free, ages 2–8).**
*Sees/does:* A character-led library, not a phonics course: the **Letters tab** (practise letter sounds with Ollo the elephant), the **Reading tab → Reading Foundational Skills** (e.g. *Blend Sounds 1, Beginning Sounds 1, Words: End Sounds* in a suggested order), tracing/letter formation, word building, and a large book library including early decodables with read-aloud + word highlighting. A personalised learning path adapts difficulty, but there is no published GPC scope-and-sequence and no mastery gating of phonics content comparable to an SSP programme. *Does best:* zero cost, no ads, breadth (phonics inside a whole-child curriculum), trustworthy for EFL families as listening/vocabulary input around a phonics core. *Limitations:* phonics is shallow and browse-organised; a child can spend months in the app without systematic GPC coverage; US voices. Sources: index, https://khankids.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/4403698893965-Phonics-The-building-blocks-of-literacy ; index, https://www.khanacademy.org/kids/ela

**IXL (US; English Language Arts).**
*Sees/does:* No story world at all: a **taxonomy of hundreds of micro-skills**, each a short adaptive question set with immediate corrective feedback and a SmartScore (0–100) mastery target — e.g. *Blend onset and rime together to make a word*, *Blend each sound in a word together*, *Identify the first, second and last sound in a word*, *Change the first/vowel/last sound in a word*, *Choose the correct digraph*. Its published Reading Foundations scope (K–5) is explicitly grounded in Scarborough's Rope, Ehri (2014) and the NRP, sequences word-level → sentence-level → decodable-text skills, and delays "irregular" high-frequency words until CVC decoding is solid. A diagnostic recommends exactly which skills to practise. *Does best:* granular skill decomposition + assessment-as-practice; the best model in this survey for *naming and measuring* every sub-skill a pattern involves. *Limitations:* it *practises and measures* far better than it *teaches* (short explainer videos only); no decodable-book integration of its own; joyless for under-7s without an adult; US. Source: index, https://www.ixl.com/materials/Reading_Foundations_Scope_and_Sequence.pdf

**Alphablocks (BBC CBeebies; TV + games, ages 3–6).**
*Sees/does:* Twenty-six living letter-characters, each with one sound and a personality. The core visual grammar is the best blending metaphor in any medium: letters **hold hands, sound out in turn ("c-a-t"), then shout the blended word ("CAT!") — and if the word is real, it *comes to life*** (a real cat appears), giving instant semantic confirmation that blending worked. Digraphs are shown by two characters **fusing into a single body**; split digraphs by **Magic E** shooting a beam to his vowel partner. Levels progress Red (letters) → Yellow (blending; first letters s a t p…) → Green (starter digraphs ch sh th ng ai ee oa oo ar or ur ow oi + trigraphs igh ear air ure) → Blue (blends/clusters — explicitly "no new sound, just blend as normal") → alternative spellings (ai *sail* vs ay *say*; ee *bee* vs ea *beat*) and gradually-introduced exception words. *Does best:* making the *mechanics* of the code visible and funny, in British pronunciation, for free, with zero failure state. *Limitations:* it is broadcast/browse media — any episode in any order, no practice loop, no assessment, no encoding (spelling) work; gains depend on an adult or a programme around it. Source: index (BBC page via mirror), https://meleleh.pages.dev/cbeebies/grownups/the-alphablocks-guide-to-phonics ; canonical BBC URL as linked by schools: https://www.bbc.co.uk/cbeebies/grownups/the-alphablocks-guide-to-phonics

**Orton-Gillingham-based apps — Nessy, Reading Horizons.**
*Sees/does (Nessy, Bristol Dyslexia Centre, ages 5–12):* OG's simultaneous see-hear-trace routine inside a game world: videos and games per pattern (e.g. igh), mnemonic stories and tricks (*necessary* = "one collar, two sleeves"; stories to fix b/d reversal), adaptive targeting of weak patterns. *Reading Horizons Discovery* is the opposite aesthetic: a faithful, explicit digital OG tutor, "school-like"/dated interface. *Does best (Nessy):* engagement for dyslexic/struggling readers who need far more repetitions than peers, without drill shame. *Limitations:* the OG brand's evidence does not support its premium claims (Stevens et al. 2021 meta-analysis: pooled OG effects **not statistically significant** — §4); game reward layers can distract from the grapheme itself. Sources: index, https://www.structural-learning.com/post/nessy ; index, https://tatyanaelleseff.com/og-limitations/

### 2. Interaction design specifics

| Technique | Best exemplar(s) | Exactly what happens | Phonics job it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tap-a-grapheme, hear-the-sound sound board** | Jolly app (per-sound audio + action image); UFLI Visual Drill slides; Teach Your Monster *Spaceship Builder* (fly over blocks to *hear* them, tap the match) | Grapheme tiles on a board; tapping speaks the pure phoneme; in drill mode a sound is played and the child taps the grapheme (sound→print and print→sound both ways) | GPC automaticity; discrimination of confusables |
| **Drag-letters-to-build-words (word building / word chains)** | **UFLI Virtual Word Work Mat** (Beginner/Intermediate tile sets); UFLI Blending Board word chains | Child drags grapheme tiles (digraphs are *single tiles*) to build the dictated word; next word differs by **one phoneme** (sat→pat→pit→pin…); child must notice *which* tile to change, re-blend, and self-check against the spoken word | Segmenting + encoding; phoneme manipulation; makes "one sound, one tile (even if 2–3 letters)" tangible |
| **Word-family / onset-rime sliders & wheels** | Reading Eggs word-family activities; GraphoGame Rime; IXL *Change one letter to make a new word* | Fixed rime (-at) with a sliding/wheel onset (c-, p-, s-…) or vice versa; each spin produces a new real word to blend | Analogy decoding; rime-unit consolidation (Ehri's consolidated phase). **Caution:** this is *analytic* phonics — powerful as consolidation, weaker as the primary teaching route than grapheme-by-grapheme synthesis |
| **Minimal-pair listening discrimination** | Lexia phonological-awareness activities; IXL *Which two words start with the same sound? / Find the word with the same vowel sound*; Khan *Beginning/End Sounds* | Audio-only prompts (no print): hear *pin* vs *pen*, choose the picture/word with the target sound; or odd-one-out among rhymes | Phonemic awareness, vowel discrimination — critical for EFL learners whose L1 lacks the contrast (e.g. /ɪ/ vs /iː/, /æ/ vs /e/ for Chinese speakers) |
| **Mouth-shape / articulation media** | 斑马《自然拼读训练》: **107 口型视频** (mouth-shape videos) + mouth diagrams in 《自然拼读全攻略》; Little Wandle pure-sound pronunciation videos; UFLI articulation cards/gestures in New Concept step; sound-wall mouth photos (§6) | Short close-up video/photo of lips-tongue-teeth for each phoneme, often with a mirror prompt ("feel your mouth"); voiced/voiceless pairs shown together (/f/ vs /v/ — same mouth, throat buzz differs) | Pronunciation accuracy + phoneme identity, especially EFL and speech difficulties; turns an invisible event (voicing, tongue position) into a discriminable cue |
| **Handwriting tracing tied to sounds** | Jolly app animated formation + finger tracing (formation phrase per letter); Khan Kids tracing; RWI formation mnemonics ("Maisie, mountain, mountain") | The letter forms stroke-by-stroke *while its sound plays*; the child traces, saying the sound — never the letter name — during formation | GPC bonding through motor memory; letter-reversal prevention. Note the evidence caveat in §4: tracing is a reasonable encoding aid, not a proven unique ingredient |
| **Gamified assessment that feels like play** | Teach Your Monster (assessment = which graphemes get repeated; child sees only adventure progress + collectible Trickies); Lexia Assessment Without Testing (invisible); Reading Eggs end-of-map quiz (the most test-like of the three, softened by rewards) | No test screen, no score shown to the child; difficulty and repetition adapt; adults get the dashboard (Teach Your Monster: strongest/weakest graphemes per child) | Measurement without test anxiety; data for gating |
| **Spaced repetition of GPCs** | Little Wandle review cadence (daily/weekly/termly + Friday review lesson); UFLI Steps 1–4 of *every* lesson = cumulative review drills before any new content; Lexia branching (failed skills recycle); Teach Your Monster repetition of weak graphemes | Previously taught GPCs are re-drilled on expanding intervals and *inside* every new lesson, not in a separate revision unit | Long-term retention/automaticity. **Gap:** no children's phonics product exposes a true SRS algorithm (Leitner/Anki-style per-item scheduling) to the learner — repetition is lesson- or level-based, not item-scheduled |

**Design rules that emerge across exemplars:** (1) Digraphs must be *single interactive units* (one tile, one fused character, one "special friend") — never two tappable letters that speak separately. (2) Every blending interaction should end in *meaning* (picture/word-comes-to-life/sentence) so the child can self-verify. (3) Encoding (building/spelling) deserves equal interaction budget to decoding — UFLI and Sounds-Write do this; most consumer apps don't. (4) Error feedback should *re-teach the exact sub-skill* (Lexia Instruction step), not just replay or mark wrong — Hooked on Phonics' slow, generic error response is the counter-example.

### 3. Sight words / high-frequency words inside phonics products

**The terminological reset (Ehri).** In the research sense, a **sight word is any word a reader recognises automatically** — *cat* becomes a sight word exactly as *said* does, through **orthographic mapping**: bonding a word's spelling to its pronunciation and meaning via phoneme segmentation + GPC knowledge (Ehri 2014, index, https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1027413). **High-frequency word** is a frequency label, not a learning method. Whole-word memorisation (flashcard shape-learning, "look and say") is therefore not a rival technique for a special word class; it is the *pre-alphabetic* strategy Ehri's phases describe children abandoning. Of the Dolch 220, only **37% are genuinely irregular "heart words"; 63% are fully decodable "flash words"** that should never have been taught by rote at all.

**The heart-word routine (UFLI; Really Great Reading "Heart Word Magic"; Farrell et al.'s model) — the current best practice, step by step:**
1. Say the word; children repeat; segment it into phonemes (tap/count sounds).
2. Map each phoneme to its grapheme *with the children*, left to right.
3. Identify the one grapheme whose sound is unexpected — **put a heart over only that part** ("In *said*, s and d are regular; only *ai* is irregular — that's the part we learn by heart").
4. Say the sounds (including the heart part's actual sound), blend, spell aloud, write it; review on a spaced schedule.
5. Distinguish **temporarily irregular** (regular, but a needed GPC hasn't been taught yet — e.g. *the* in UFLI Lesson 1 carries hearts on both *th* and *e*; once *th* is taught, only *e* remains a heart part on the permanent card) from **permanently irregular** (a correspondence unique to that word or a small family — *oul* in could/would/should, taught as a family with a shared mnemonic).
Sources: index, https://elvis.padletcdn.com/1/fetch/c_a/padlet-artifacts.storage.googleapis.com/6dbdd08bb673cf9f70d4fc1e81f592a98b44733d/1c3cff0fcc6fd7f7e0b0ce3f82876054-h-715760cfb83aa49a7f60cfc52a3742d4.pdf (UFLI Irregular Word Cards instructions) ; index, https://www.reallygreatreading.com/blog/make-tricky-sight-words-sticky-really-great-reading-blog ; index, https://www.Lexialearning.com/blog/alphabetic-phase-theory-4-steps-to-literacy-success

**How each product family reconciles irregulars with decodable teaching:**
- **UFLI / Really Great Reading:** heart-word mapping as above, *inside* the phonics lesson (UFLI Step 7 of every lesson), sequenced so most "irregularity" is temporary. Really Great Reading additionally renames the regular ones (Simple/Flash heart words in its usage) to stop teachers flashcard-teaching decodable words.
- **Little Wandle / Letters and Sounds:** "tricky words" are defined *relative to the child's current code* ("cannot be decoded using the GPCs taught up to that point… many tricky words cease to be tricky later") — the temporary-irregular concept, without the heart visual.
- **RWI:** "Red Words" taught in a separate strand; older parent guidance frames them as pure memorisation (weakest model here — see §1.2).
- **Jolly:** "Tricky Words" as a third app route alongside Letter Sounds and Alternatives — looked at as wholes with the tricky letter noted; mid-way between whole-word and mapping approaches.
- **Teach Your Monster:** Trickies as collectible characters, learned by repeated exposure/feeding games — engaging, but whole-word recognition, not mapping.
- **IXL:** sequencing solution — irregular HFWs are simply *withheld* until CVC decoding is secure, then introduced as "patterns that may be unknown".
- **Reading Eggs core:** sight words introduced early alongside phonics (Maps 1–4 already include *the, I, and, is, see*) — the pragmatic consumer compromise, and the one most at odds with the mapping literature.

**Design takeaway:** irregularity should be rendered as a *property of a grapheme inside a word* (heart/highlight on the grapheme, with its actual pronunciation spoken), never as a property of the whole word; and the product should *retire* hearts automatically when the relevant GPC is later taught.

### 4. Research evidence

**Systematic phonics works; the brand wars don't.**
- **National Reading Panel (2000):** systematic phonics vs alternatives, **mean ES = 0.44**; by outcome (Ehri et al. 2001 analysis): regular-word decoding d = 0.67, pseudowords 0.60, irregular words 0.40, spelling 0.35, comprehension 0.27–0.51. **No significant difference between phonics types** (synthetic, larger-unit/analytic, miscellaneous: ~0.27–0.45) or delivery modes (tutoring 0.57, small group 0.43, whole class 0.39); effects strongest in K–Grade 1 and for at-risk readers (index, https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/286a03c7-4785-4862-90de-62437ed45f82).
- **Rose Review (2006):** recommended high-quality phonic work — specifically the synthetic model (GPCs in a clearly defined incremental sequence; blend all through the word; segment to spell; the two skills as reversible processes) — as the **prime** approach, taught discretely *within* a broad, language-rich curriculum, replacing the "searchlights" model with the Simple View of Reading.
- **EEF Teaching and Learning Toolkit:** phonics = **+5 months' progress, moderate impact, very low cost, "extensive" evidence**; improves reading *accuracy* more reliably than comprehension; particularly beneficial for disadvantaged and younger (4–7) pupils (index, https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/teaching-learning-toolkit/phonics).
- **Castles, Rastle & Nation (2018), "Ending the Reading Wars":** the field's synthesis — phonics is central *because* English is alphabetic, but is "necessary, not sufficient": oral language, fluency, morphology and comprehension instruction must surround it (index, https://admin.jet.org.za/clearinghouse/projects/primted/resources/language-and-literacy-resources-repository/castles-et-al-2018.pdf).
- **The dissent, fairly stated:** Jeffrey Bowers argues the meta-analytic headline overstates the case — NRP's overall effect is small (d = 0.41 immediate, **0.27 delayed**; text reading 0.25, comprehension 0.27), England's Phonics Screening Check gains have not visibly transferred to PIRLS reading scores, and Northern Ireland (no mandated SSP/PSC) outperformed England — and proposes Structured Word Inquiry instead (index, https://dyslexiaguild.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/Guild-lecture-Bowers-June-2019-v2.pdf). Buckingham's rebuttal ("evidence strongly favours systematic synthetic phonics") disputes Bowers' re-analyses (index, https://www.ldaustralia.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/Buckingham-Evidence-strongly-favours-systematic-synthetic-phonics.pdf). A product designer should take from this dispute: *phonics-vs-nothing is settled; synthetic-vs-other-systematic and long-term comprehension transfer are not* — don't over-claim either in product copy.

**Ehri's phases as a product-spec.** Pre-alphabetic (visual-cue guessing) → Partial alphabetic (first/final-letter connections; guessing from partial cues) → Full alphabetic (complete GPC connections; true decoding and mapping) → Consolidated alphabetic (multi-letter units — rimes, syllables, morphemes — recognised as chunks). Software implications: (i) activities that reward first-letter guessing (many "which word is this?" picture games) *train the partial phase*; (ii) word-family wheels and morpheme work belong to the *consolidated* phase — as consolidation, not introduction; (iii) assessment should detect *phase behaviours* (guessing vs full blending), as Lexia's branching implicitly does.

**Decodable vs leveled text — the honest summary is "thinner evidence than either camp admits."** Shanahan's review: only a handful of studies since the 1980s, with no agreed definition of "decodable"; Mesmer (2005) found children *attempt decoding more* with decodable text, Mesmer (2010) found *greater fluency* with less-decodable leveled text; Cheatham & Allor's (2012) review concludes decodables support applying phonics, **mainly during a brief window in Grade 1**; a later meta-analysis found programmes *combining* decodable with other texts beat only-decodable, no-decodable, or no-text conditions. Shanahan's recommendation: use decodables as phonics practice, in a **limited dose**, inside a mix of text types (index, https://www.readingrockets.org/blogs/shanahan-on-literacy/should-we-teach-decodable-text). Product designs that embody this well: Little Wandle (decodable matched to *secure* code + separate "reading for pleasure" books shared by adults) and Hooked on Phonics (a decodable per lesson + encouragement to supplement). Designs that over-reach: marketing that treats months of 100%-decodable text as a complete reading diet.

**App-based phonics — trial evidence, product by product:**
- **Lexia Core5 — the strongest file.** RCT (Northern Ireland, O'Callaghan et al. 2016, *Br. J. Educ. Psychol.*): 98 below-average 4–6-year-olds randomised to 8 weeks of daily Core5 (~648 min total) vs waitlist; significant gains in **blending (partial η² = .064)** and **nonword reading (η² = .070)** — *small* effects, maintained at 2-month follow-up; gains were not uniform. EEF's larger **effectiveness trial** (struggling readers, school-delivered): **+2 months' reading progress** vs comparison, 4-padlock (high) security rating, with word identification, phonics application and comprehension all positive; EAL pupils +3 months on a small subgroup (interpret with caution) (index, https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/projects-and-evaluation/promising-programmes/lexia-reading-core5).
- **GraphoGame / GraphoGame Rime:** the Finnish original (transparent orthography) shows solid gains for at-risk readers (Saine et al. 2011: letter naming, fluency, spelling). The **English UK RCT is the cautionary tale**: the headline result was weak, and the published efficacy paper analyses only the **"top half" of players** (those who played above the mean progress point, n = 95), finding phonics gains vs business-as-usual in that compliant subgroup and in IEP/younger/"requires improvement" school subgroups — i.e. *the game works for children who actually play it enough*, a dose/compliance finding, not a blanket effect (index, https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2020.00132/full).
- **Reading Eggs:** the cited study (Lowery 2017) is a 31-child, 6-week, two-classroom action-research project using the Fountas & Pinnell benchmark (itself a criticised measure); the programme's ESSA Level III standing rests substantially on this. Treat "93% improve a reading level in 6 weeks" as marketing, not trial evidence (index, https://assets.readingeggs.com/mkt/research-reports/re_evaluation_lowery_2017.pdf ; critique: https://www.phonics.org/reading-eggs-app-review-for-phonics/).
- **Teach Your Monster to Read:** **no published RCT of the game itself was found** in this research; the developer commendably says it is building a stronger evidence base into the next version. Precise-sounding claims circulating on content sites ("1,000+ students, +1.5 grade levels in 12 weeks", "University of Sussex 2017, +20%") could not be traced to any peer-reviewed source and should be treated as **unverified** (index, https://mediataylor.com/firm-but-unfair-the-spectators-war-on-edtech/).
- **Across apps:** a PRISMA systematic review of electronic reading interventions found 55 RCT/quasi-experimental studies covering 33 tools — highly heterogeneous, mostly medium risk of bias, with a call for samples >65/group and interventions >15 hours before drawing conclusions (index, https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.652948/full). *General rule: the pedagogy (systematic phonics) is evidence-based; almost no individual app is.*

**Multisensory claims — scrutiny.** Orton-Gillingham is the test case because it brands itself "multisensory". Stevens et al. (2021) meta-analysis (24 studies from 109 articles): OG interventions produced **no statistically significant effects** on word reading, phonics, fluency, vocabulary or comprehension (foundational-skills ES = 0.32, ns). Schlesinger & Gray (2017) directly compared structured literacy with and without the multisensory components: **no added benefit** for the multisensory version on any outcome, including for dyslexic learners. The defensible reading: what works inside OG/Jolly-style programmes is the *explicit, systematic, cumulative* phonics; tracing in sand, air-writing and action-per-sound are **engagement and mnemonic devices without isolated causal evidence** — harmless and often delightful, but they should never displace blending practice time, and one major programme (Little Wandle) omits songs/actions *on purpose*, citing cognitive overload (sources in §1.2; index, https://tatyanaelleseff.com/og-limitations/ ; index, https://www.structural-learning.com/post/nessy).

**After the GPC basics: morphology and etymology.** English spelling is *morphophonemic* — it preserves meaning units at the expense of sound transparency (*sign/signal*, the *ph* of *phone* from Greek). Two handoff designs exist: (i) **Phonics-programme extension** — UFLI Lessons 119–128 teach affixes as decoding units (-sion/-tion, -ture, agentive -er/-or/-ist, bi-/tri-/uni-) and UFLI sells a morphology sequel (*Word Origins*); Lexia's "structural analysis" strand does the same inside software; Sounds-Write teaches polysyllabic words alongside the Extended Code. (ii) **Structured Word Inquiry (Bowers & Kirby)** — investigate a word family with a **matrix** (a grid that generates all words from a base + prefixes/suffixes) and **word sums** (base + suffix spelled out with conventions, e.g. *sign + al → signal* explains the silent g); four questions: What does it mean? How is it built? What are its relatives? How do its graphemes work? Bowers & Kirby (2010, Grades 4/5) found SWI pupils better at **defining untaught words sharing a taught base** — generative vocabulary transfer (index, https://structuredwordinquiry.com/about/ ; index, https://research-information.bris.ac.uk/ws/files/101499682/bowers_and_bowers.in_press.pdf). The matrix is, notably, a *complete-inventory* visualisation — every combination shown — which consumer phonics products never offer (see §9).

**Song/chant reliance.** Across the Chinese products (and Jolly), songs are not decoration but the *primary memory mechanism*: 叽里呱啦 ends each phonics lesson with a chant that serialises the rules; 斑马/洪恩 front-load 儿歌 for letter sounds. This suits a market where lessons are short, parent-mediated and homework is choral repetition — but it risks exactly what Little Wandle warns about (melody and movement consuming working memory) and what Chinese critics call 唱会了规则、读不出文章 (can sing the rule, can't read the text).

**The criticism, stated precisely.** (1) **Prerequisite inversion:** L1 phonics maps *known spoken words* onto print (concept → sound → print). The typical Chinese beginner has near-zero English hearing vocabulary, so the mapping runs print → sound → *nothing* — decoding without a meaning to land on. Hence the widely cited guidance attributed to 盖兆泉 and repeated across Chinese educator sources: build **1,000–3,000 hearing words first** (some say at least 300–500), start systematic phonics around age 6. (2) **"先记规则后套词" (rules first, words as exercises afterwards):** rules are taught as declarative knowledge (there are ~185 commonly listed rules; Chinese courses typically teach ~80) and drilled on isolated words, with insufficient reading volume for the rules to proceduralise — VIPKid's misconceptions guide lists the canonical five errors: treating phonics as a 万能钥匙 (master key), starting too early, fearing pinyin confusion, **只背规则不读书 (memorising rules without reading)**, and treating phonics as a finished stage （学完就扔） (index, https://www.vipkid.com.cn/posts/30049.html). (3) **Exception overload as marketing backlash:** the -ough family (tough/though/through/bough/cough/thorough) is Exhibit A in Chinese sceptical writing that "见词能读、听音能写" is oversold (index, https://www.douban.com/note/787591857/ ; index, https://www.sina.cn/news/detail/5272494454875876.html). The fair synthesis: phonics is *necessary but radically insufficient* for EFL readers, and EFL products must co-teach the *meaning* of every word they ask children to decode — 叽里呱啦's story-first loop and ABCmouse.cn's listening-first staging are the two Chinese designs that visibly respond to this.

### 6. Presentation conventions: organising the pattern inventory

**Three browsing axes exist in the wild, and products pick at most two:**
1. **By grapheme (print → sound).** Jolly app (Letter Sounds list, then Alternatives), RWI Speed Sound cards, Letters and Sounds phases, Teach Your Monster stages. Natural question answered: *"What does **ea** say?"* — but the answer is a *list of lessons over time*, never a single screen.
2. **By phoneme (sound → print).** **Sound walls**: 44 phonemes organised by articulation — consonants by place/manner (stops, fricatives…; voiced/voiceless pairs sharing a card), vowels by mouth position — each phoneme card showing a **mouth photo** and *all its graphemes in frequency order*. Contrasted explicitly with **word walls** (alphabetical by first letter: adult logic, print-to-speech, promotes shape memorisation, useless for spelling *know* — you'd look under N). Sources: index, https://www.ldatschool.ca/shifting-from-word-walls-to-sound-walls/ ; index, https://pld-literacy.org/sound-walls-structured-synthetic-phonics-programs/. **Phonics International's Alphabetic Code Chart** is the print/digital reference version: rows = phonemes, each row = *every* grapheme for that sound with a key picture and key word, simple code at left shading into complex code. **Sounds-Write's Extended Code** teaches in this direction (one phoneme, all its spellings per unit).
3. **By word family / rime.** Reading Eggs, GraphoGame Rime, classroom word-family wheels: -at, -ight, -ough as units. Natural for Ehri's consolidated phase and for analogy decoding; dangerous as the *primary* organisation because rime families hide their own exceptions (-ough again).

**Does any product show ALL the words a pattern covers? — Essentially, no (with two partial exceptions).**
- Learning products show **curated samples only**: RWI Green Word lists per sound, Jolly's word bank, UFLI's word chains and Roll-and-Read sheets, Teach Your Monster's in-game word sets, Reading Eggs' lesson words. Curation is deliberate (only use words whose *other* GPCs are already taught) — but it means the learner never sees the *extent* or the *boundaries* of a pattern, and exceptions appear as surprises rather than as a visible minority subset.
- **Phonics International's Phonics Spelling Dictionary / Code Chart** comes closest on the *grapheme* side: for each phoneme it lists the full grapheme inventory with key words — but key words are still exemplars, not the complete word list per grapheme.
- **IXL** comes closest on the *skill* side (exhaustive skill inventory), with exemplar words per skill.
- No product found — Anglo or Chinese — renders, for a pattern like *ea → /iː/*, the **complete set of word-bank words containing it**, let alone partitions a grapheme's words by pronunciation. This is a genuine white space (see §9).

**British (RP-ish) vs American pronunciation.** The market splits cleanly by origin: **British** — Teach Your Monster (explicitly British articulation; its /r/ is non-rhotic), Jolly, RWI, Little Wandle, Alphablocks, Sounds-Write (with an explicit note that regional pronunciations are acceptable in the Phonics Screening Check — e.g. *ea* in *head* = /ee/ in some regions). **American** — Lexia, IXL, Khan Kids, Hooked on Phonics, ABCmouse, and effectively all Chinese-market products （斑马's research volume specifies 美音 videos). **Australian** — Reading Eggs, UFLI's Australian edition (a separate Toolbox localisation — proof that accent localisation is operationally feasible). Accent is not cosmetic in phonics: rhoticity changes the entire r-controlled vowel set (ar/er/ir/or/ur), the BATH/TRAP split changes *a* in *grass/path*, and LOT/CLOTH differences change *o* — a product teaching "the sound of ar" is teaching *different GPCs* in RP and General American. Almost no product offers an accent toggle; UFLI's separate US/AUS toolboxes are the exception that proves the rule.

**Variant pronunciations and exceptions — how products handle *ea* → /iː/ vs /e/ vs /eɪ/:**
- **Sounds-Write:** a dedicated **alternative-pronunciation unit**: *ea* in **head** (/e/), **steam** (/iː/), **steak** (/eɪ/) taught together, explicitly, after the main correspondences — the variant set *is* the lesson.
- **Phonics International:** the chart annotates rarity: *ea* as code for /eɪ/ is flagged "rare — note the three common root words: **break, steak, great**". Frequency honesty as a design element.
- **Letters and Sounds Phase 5 / Little Wandle Phase 5 / Teach Your Monster Stage 3:** alternative pronunciations arrive as a *phase/stage* ("i in fin vs find"), i.e. variants are sequenced late and globally, not attached to the grapheme's home screen.
- **Jolly:** the app's "Alternatives" route groups a letter's other sounds — the closest consumer-app equivalent.
- **Weakest handling:** products that teach one sound per grapheme and let exceptions surface only inside "tricky words" (early Reading Eggs; RWI Red Words) — the child experiences the variant as a *word* problem, not a *grapheme* property.
- **Best-practice synthesis:** a grapheme page should show its pronunciations **ranked by frequency with their word sets partitioned** (ea→/iː/: *eat, sea, dream…* (the large default set); ea→/e/: *head, bread, deaf…* (a learnable closed set); ea→/eɪ/: *great, break, steak* (a 3-word closed set)) — converting "exceptions" into *small complete sets*, which are memorisable precisely because they are complete. No surveyed product does this with real word counts; Phonics International's rarity notes gesture at it.

### 9. Gaps — aspects NO product handles well

1. **Complete per-pattern word inventories.** Every learning product curates samples; none shows *all* words (in any defined corpus/word bank) that a pattern covers, with counts. Learners therefore can't see a pattern's true productivity ("*ai* appears in 200+ common words") or its true exception rate. Reference charts (Phonics International) complete the *grapheme* inventory per phoneme, not the *word* inventory per pattern.
2. **Grapheme variant networks.** Variants are handled as late phases (L&S P5), single lessons (Sounds-Write ea), or rarity footnotes (PI) — never as a persistent, navigable network: grapheme ↔ its pronunciations (frequency-ranked, word sets partitioned) ↔ other graphemes sharing each pronunciation. The two directions of the code are never cross-linked in one learner-facing graph.
3. **Morphology handoff.** Phonics products end (UFLI affixes and Lexia structural analysis are honourable partial exceptions); SWI products start (at Grade 4/5, classroom-based, no software of comparable polish). No product carries a child *continuously* from GPCs → syllable types → morphemes → etymology with the same inventory/data model.
4. **RP-specific products.** British-accent phonics exists in *programmes and media* (RWI, Little Wandle, Alphablocks, Teach Your Monster) but the adaptive/software-rich end of the market (Lexia, IXL, Khan, Hooked) is entirely American, and the EFL market defaults to American even in the UK-oriented curriculum's former colonies. No product offers an accent toggle despite accent changing the GPC set itself (rhotic vowels, BATH words). UFLI's separate US/AUS toolboxes show localisation is possible but nobody does RP-vs-GA as a setting.
5. **EFL-specific pacing.** L1 programmes assume the words being decoded are already in the child's oral vocabulary. No surveyed product — including the Chinese ones, which *stage* listening first at course level — enforces this *per word*: i.e. checks that a word is orally known (or teaches its meaning inline) before demanding it be decoded, tracks hearing-vocabulary vs decoding-vocabulary separately, or targets L1-specific phoneme contrasts （对中文孩子： /ɪ/–/iː/, /æ/–/e/, /v/–/w/, final consonants, consonant clusters) as first-class pattern types. 叽里呱啦's story-first lesson loop is the closest gesture.
6. **True spaced repetition.** Review is lesson-scheduled (UFLI/Little Wandle) or weakness-triggered (Teach Your Monster, Lexia), never item-scheduled with per-GPC forgetting curves — despite GPCs being an ideal SRS domain (small, finite, testable item set: ~175 correspondences).
7. **Assessment of *phase behaviour*.** Products measure accuracy; none explicitly diagnoses Ehri-phase behaviours (first-letter guessing, partial blending) and prescribes the corresponding activity type.
8. **Encoding parity in consumer apps.** Classroom programmes (UFLI, Sounds-Write) treat spelling/building as half the work; consumer apps overwhelmingly test recognition and reading, with building/spelling as garnish.

### 10. Direct answer: the best design model for a word-bank-complete phonics module in British pronunciation

**Question restated:** a phonics module whose word sets are *derived completely from a word bank* — every word matching a pattern appears — in British pronunciation. Which single product, or blend, is the best design model?

**No single product qualifies** — because the defining requirement (completeness) is the industry's shared blind spot (§9.1–9.2), and the strongest inventory models are classroom/reference artefacts, not software. **The best model is a four-part blend:**

1. **Inventory architecture — Sounds-Write Extended Code × Phonics International Alphabetic Code Chart.** Organise the module *by phoneme* as the primary spine (44 RP phonemes; each phoneme page lists every grapheme that spells it, frequency-ordered, with mouth media), cross-indexed *by grapheme* (each grapheme page lists its pronunciations, frequency-ranked). This is the only architecture in which "every word matching a pattern" is even definable in both directions — a *pattern* is a (grapheme, pronunciation) pair, and its page is the complete word-bank partition for that pair: *ea–/iː/* (large default set), *ea–/e/* (complete small set: head, bread, deaf…), *ea–/eɪ/* (complete tiny set: great, break, steak). Completeness converts exceptions from embarrassments into closed sets with visible sizes — the single most valuable pedagogical consequence of the word-bank design, and one no competitor offers.
2. **Lesson & practice loop — UFLI.** Wrap each pattern in the 8-step rhythm reduced to software: hear/see drill → blending board (words built live from the pattern's own word list, in word chains) → word-work building (learner drags tiles; digraphs as single tiles) → heart-word mapping for the pattern's irregular members → a short decodable text generated/selected from *only* the taught patterns + the complete current set. UFLI's routines are free, published and proven at classroom scale; nothing else matches their blend/build/read density.
3. **Child-facing shell & voice — Teach Your Monster to Read (verified live) + Alphablocks' visual grammar.** British voices throughout (Teach Your Monster is the only top-tier game that is natively British); monster/quest motivation and collectible pattern characters for the adventure layer; Alphablocks' hold-hands/fusion/Magic-E animations as the *explanatory* language for blending, digraphs and split digraphs inside lessons. Sequence the adventure on a Letters-and-Sounds/Little-Wandle-style progression (the progression Teach Your Monster already mirrors), with Little Wandle's review cadence (daily/weekly/termly + a weekly review session) as the retention engine.
4. **Gating & assessment — Lexia's pattern, on a hybrid-gate chassis (Hooked on Phonics).** Strict mastery gating on the *teaching spine* (new patterns unlock on demonstrated blending/building accuracy, with Lexia-style branching: error → micro-re-teach → retry; repeated failure → adult flag + prescribed offline activity), **but with the complete inventory pages always freely browsable** (Hooked's path-plus-library hybrid). This resolves the completeness-vs-curation tension that stops other products showing everything: *practice* uses curated, code-controlled subsets (only words whose other GPCs are taught — decodability discipline), while *reference/browsing* shows the complete set, with not-yet-decodable words visibly marked as "coming later" rather than hidden. Assessment stays invisible (per-GPC accuracy model from normal play); adults see the grapheme-level dashboard.

**British-pronunciation specifics to lock in the data model** (where the blend must *not* copy its American parts): non-rhotic r-controlled vowels (ar = /ɑː/, er/ir/ur = /ɜː/, or = /ɔː/ as single vowel phonemes, not vowel+/r/); BATH set (*grass, path, dance* with /ɑː/) as a named variant partition of *a*; LOT–CLOTH and *o* conventions per RP; *ea* in *head* regional note (Sounds-Write accepts regional variants in the Phonics Screening Check — a complete word bank should tag, not hide, regional pronunciations). Record/human-voice audio per phoneme and per word — synthetic TTS accent drift is a real risk for a product whose promise is pronunciation.

**What to deliberately leave out:** Jolly-style action-per-sound as a *required* layer (offer songs/actions as optional mnemonic garnish; the evidence doesn't support them as core, and Little Wandle's cognitive-overload objection is credible for software where the child is alone); whole-word tricky-word memorisation (use heart-word mapping, §3); onset-rime as the primary organisation (keep word families as a *view* on the inventory — it falls out of the data for free — not as the teaching spine); and any marketing claim of trial-proven efficacy for the blend itself (the components are evidence-based; the combination, like almost every app in §4, would not be).
