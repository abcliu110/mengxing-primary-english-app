# The Interface Is the Content: How Excellent Verb/Tense Products Make Tense-Aspect Visible, Navigable and Manipulable

*Re-centred deliverable (binding steering, 2026-11-02). Curriculum sequence and exercise types are included only where they are embodied in an interface — a path, a map, a page layout, a widget. Prior-turn sequence research is in `notes/sources.md`; interface sources for this turn are in `notes/interface-sources.md`.*

## Summary — the thesis, in one paragraph

Every excellent product answers three interface questions differently from a lecture list, and the differences *are* the pedagogy. **(1) What shape is the whole system on screen?** The best shapes are a **matrix** (Azar's Summary Chart, Lingolia's timeline: time × aspect, one constant verb — the system's *relations* are visible) or a **matrix with a route threaded through it**. The most popular shape, Duolingo's **path**, shows only *progress* — where you are, never what the system is — and its nodes are labelled by topic, so the tense system is literally invisible on the home screen. **(2) What is on one unit's screen, in what order?** In every strong unit the screen is *zoned by cognitive job* and practice is on the same surface as presentation (Murphy's facing pages; Lingolia's dialogue→uses→form; British Council's test→explain→retest sandwich). Where practice is exiled to another page/tab (Perfect English Grammar), the unit degenerates into reading. **(3) Which elements can the learner *move*?** The elements that teach are the ones whose manipulation *is* a content distinction: clicking an aspect row in Lingolia changes the glyph on the timeline; picking slots in a sentence builder composes the form; removing the time adverb in a structured-input drill forces the verb ending to carry the meaning. A slider that only changes a label ("Past Perfect!") while an explanation paragraph changes underneath is a lecture station wearing a widget costume — which is the current app's problem, stated in interface terms.

**Confidence:** Lingolia (timeline + unit page), Perfect English Grammar and the IIENSTITU builder were **verified live** in the research window this turn, including clicking Lingolia's Progressive row. Murphy/Azar print interfaces are from publisher tables of contents, sample spreads and chart reproductions (index). Duolingo/Busuu/Babbel/BBC in-app screens could not be opened signed-out; they are described from reviews, course-information documents and Duolingo's own research report (index) — flagged per item.

## 1. The whole curriculum as an interface: five shapes for "the system"

What the learner *sees* as the shape of tense-aspect determines what they think tense-aspect *is*: a journey, a territory, a list, or a machine with switches.

| # | Shape | Exemplar | What is on the home/contents screen | What it teaches about the system | Its blindness |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Winding path** (progress map) | **Duolingo** (2022– ) | One vertical winding path; coloured banner per unit; circular lesson nodes with icons; a progress *ring* around the current node; Stories and review lessons are nodes *in* the path; a Guidebook icon at each unit's start | "Language is a journey with a next step; you are *here*" | Nodes are labelled by communicative topic (*Talk about music preferences*), never by tense. There is no screen on which the verb system appears as a system. Sequence exists, structure doesn't |
| 2 | **Matrix / grid** (system map) | **Azar Summary Chart; Lingolia Timeline; IIENSTITU dashboard** | Columns = time (past/present/future), rows = aspect (simple/progressive/perfect/perfect-progressive); every cell holds the *same verb* in that form + a timeline glyph | "A tense = one time choice × one viewpoint choice; neighbours differ by exactly one switch" | By itself it has no route, no difficulty gradient, no mastery state — it is a map without a journey |
| 3 | **List + diagnostic router** | **Murphy, *English Grammar in Use*** | A contents list of 145 units in tense blocks (1–6 present/past, 7–18 perfect block, 19–25 future); a *Study Guide* diagnostic at the back routes you to units; the preface states units are **not** in difficulty order | "Grammar is a reference territory you enter at your problem" | Relations between units are invisible in the list itself; they are repaired inside units by margin cross-references (➜ Units 11–12) — a printed hyperlink network |
| 4 | **Level shelves** | **Busuu, Babbel, Lingoda** | Courses tab = shelves labelled A1/A2/B1/B2(/C1); inside a shelf, chapters in a linear feed (Busuu: tabs *Learn \| Community \| Review*) | "Grammar belongs to a proficiency band; the band is the address" | The shelf tells you *when*, not *how forms relate*; two forms on different shelves (past simple A2, present perfect B1) never appear on one screen until a dedicated contrast lesson |
| 5 | **Topic windows** | **BBC Learning English** | Course home = 30 numbered units whose titles are topics (*The Titanic, The bucket list, Great achievers*); each unit = 5 session tabs | "Grammar lives inside stories and topics" | The tense curriculum is *hidden*: present perfect is inside "Great achievers", past continuous inside "The Titanic". No tense-indexed entry point exists on the home screen |

**Key findings embodied in these interfaces**

- **Azar is the only mainstream product that shows the whole system *before* teaching any part of it.** Chapter 1 is *Overview of Verb Tenses*, ending in §1-5 *Summary Chart of Verb Tenses*; only then do chapters on present/past, perfect, and future follow, with a dedicated Review chapter after them. A separate **Chartbook** product strips out all exercises and sells the charts alone — proof that the chart *is* regarded as an interface in its own right, not decoration. (5th-ed. contents via publisher listings; Azar-derived chart reproductions show each cell = one example with the same verb + a time-span gloss, e.g. simple present *"I study every day (habitual action – past to present to future)"*.)
- **Duolingo deliberately traded the tree for the path, and its own research says the *interface* change changed outcomes.** Duolingo Research Report DRR-24-04 evaluates the 2022 linear-path redesign against the old skill tree: in the path, (i) Stories became obligatory nodes instead of an optional tab, (ii) dedicated review lessons were inserted as nodes, (iii) three difficulty tiers became obligatory — low tier ≈ recognition exercises, high tier ≈ recall/production over the *same* material. The path took longer and produced better proficiency outcomes than the tree. Note what the redesign did *not* do: it never made grammar itself visible — grammar was simultaneously *demoted* to a per-unit **Guidebook** (bite-size tips, tappable, re-openable) that reviews consistently describe as optional-feeling and thinner than the old per-skill Tips.
- **The consensus teaching sequence survives only as interface order.** Murphy's list order (present continuous → present simple → their contrast, units 3–4 → past simple → past continuous → present perfect ×2 → perfect continuous → *for/since* → present-perfect-vs-past contrast, units 13–14 → past perfect → future last, units 19–25, with present-for-future *before* *going to* and *will*), English File's file order, and the CEFR shelves (British Council/EQUALS Core Inventory: A1 present simple/continuous; A2 past simple, past continuous, basic present perfect, *will/going to*; B1 past perfect, present perfect continuous, the perfect/past contrast) all agree on the spine — but a learner only ever experiences that agreement as *which node/shelf/page comes next*. The Chinese comparison is likewise an interface fact: the 2022 national standard's Level 1 (grades 3–4) names only present simple + present continuous; primary PEP scope is four tenses; 人教版 junior moves past simple to Grade 7下 (Units 11–12) and does not give present perfect a home until Grade 8下 (Units 8–10) — i.e. in the Chinese textbook interface, present perfect has *no address* for roughly the first five years, then arrives as three consecutive units at once.

## 2. Anatomy of ONE unit's interface

Six unit screens, dissected in the order things appear, with what is manipulable and where practice lives. **The pattern: the further practice is from presentation on the screen, the weaker the unit.**

### 2.1 Murphy — the two-page spread (print's best interface)
- **Left page (read):** zones A–D. Each zone *opens with a pictured example situation* (a mini-scene: someone looking out of a window, two clock faces for past perfect) and a timeline/clock drawing, *then* the rule in plain English, *then* examples. Cross-reference arrows in the margin (➜ Unit 7, ➜ Units 13–14) are printed hyperlinks to the contrast units.
- **Right page (do):** exercises only, graded *within the page*: complete-the-form → write sentences from pictures → write sentences from situations (e.g. for present perfect: *"Tom is looking for his key. He can't find it." → Tom has lost his key* — the situation *is* a present result, so the form is forced by the scene, not by a verb in brackets) → correct-the-sentence → final exercise: *write about yourself*.
- **Interface verdict:** presentation and practice are *simultaneously visible* (an open spread). Meaning (scene) precedes form (rule) inside each zone. Errors are anticipated in the correct-the-sentence slot. Nothing is manipulable, but nothing needs scrolling either — the whole unit fits one field of view.

### 2.2 Azar — warm-up, then chart, then work
- **Order on the page:** a **Warm-Up exercise precedes the chart** (the learner attempts examples before being told anything) → the **chart**: a single table whose columns are form / examples / meaning-with-timeline-drawing → exercises including listening, pair interviews, and an **editing (error-correction) task with an editing checklist** at chapter end.
- **Interface verdict:** the chart is the unit's home screen — a compact, returnable visual object — and the warm-up makes the first screen an *attempt*, not a lecture. Azar Interactive digitised exactly this: pop-up charts available during exercises, i.e. the chart follows the learner into practice instead of staying on the presentation page.

### 2.3 Lingolia — one long page, dialogue first, form in the middle, exercises behind a door *(verified live)*
Present-perfect page, top to bottom: one-line definition ("connects the past with the present") → **a dialogue (Ada & James planning a party) in which every target use occurs in situ** → "When to use" list (result / experience with *ever, never* / *just* / *already, yet* / how-many-times / *for, since* states), each with an example lifted from the dialogue → signal-word list → **conjugation table only now** (positive/negative/question), participle spelling rules → a boxed contrast (*been* vs *gone*, decided by "where is the person *now*?") → contractions. Exercises live on a **separate linked page**, graded (gap-fill → sentence building → mixed-tense sets).
- **Interface verdict:** meaning-first is *visible in scroll order* — you must pass the dialogue and the uses to reach the form table. The cost: practice is one click away from presentation, and the page itself contains no manipulable element; manipulation is reserved for the Timeline page (§3.1), which is a *separate destination* from any unit.

### 2.4 Perfect English Grammar — the split-screen anti-example *(verified live)*
- The site nav itself splits **EXPLANATIONS | EXERCISES** into two silos. The present perfect is *two URLs*: a **MAKE** page (form) and a **USE** page (meaning) — the USE page's first element is a link out: *"Click here to learn how to MAKE the present perfect."*
- The USE page body is a read-only numbered list of five uses with bolded examples, one genuinely interface-like element — a **struck-through error** (*NOT: ~~I've seen him yesterday~~*) — and a *been/gone* section. Practice exists only as outbound links at the very bottom ("Try an exercise … here").
- **Interface verdict:** this is the closest public analogue to the Chinese app's current design — a lecture station with links. Splitting MAKE and USE into separate pages *sounds* like "meaning first, form last", but in interface terms it means the learner never sees meaning and form on one surface, and can finish the unit having manipulated nothing.

### 2.5 British Council LearnEnglish — the test–explain–retest sandwich *(index)*
One page, in order: hook examples (*I've broken my leg; Have you ever been to Scotland?*) → **"Try this exercise to test your grammar" (a test *before* any explanation)** → Grammar explanation, zoned by meaning (unfinished time & states / finished time = past simple / past actions with a result in the present / *just, yet, already*) → **"Do this exercise to test your grammar again"** (Grammar test 2). The A2/B1 perfect-simple-vs-continuous page puts the contrast in a **two-column table whose column headers are the content distinction** (focuses on result | focuses on activity; says *how many* | says *how long*).
- **Interface verdict:** the unit's first and last screens are the *same kind of act* (answer questions), with explanation sandwiched between — the interface itself tells the learner "explanation is what changes your score". This is the cheapest strong pattern to copy: it needs no new widget, only reordering.

### 2.6 App lessons — Duolingo, Babbel, Busuu (index)
- **Duolingo lesson screen:** a single exercise per screen, progress bar at top, immediate green/red full-screen feedback; exercise types escalate *within the path position*, not within the lesson (recognition early, recall/production at higher tiers). Grammar explanation is *not on the lesson screen at all* — it lives in the unit Guidebook, one tap away from the path, i.e. read-only and out of the practice loop. Stories are the exception: a story screen is a dialogue player — line-by-line audio/text, tap-a-word glosses, and a comprehension question interrupting every few lines, so checking is *inside* the story, not after it.
- **Babbel lesson screen:** dialogue first (seen + heard), then vocabulary and **grammar callout boxes extracted from that dialogue**, then matching/translation/typing with speech recognition and immediate correction. The grammar callout is an *overlay on content already met*, the opposite of a pre-lecture.
- **Busuu:** a grammar lesson is a short screen flow — 2–3 sentence explanation + examples + drills — recurring every 3–4 lessons inside the Learn feed; but Busuu's distinctive interface is *outside* the lesson: the **Review tab**, where every met word/grammar point reappears colour-coded by strength, plus a Mistake Repair list generated from your own errors. Mastery is a *visible colour on an item*, not a completed-station tick.
- **BBC session screen:** a topic unit's grammar lives in one of five session tabs (6 Minute Grammar = audio + transcript + end-of-programme quiz), with a **"Session Grammar" reference box pinned at the bottom of every session page** and a link to the full grammar reference — a persistent mini-chart under otherwise narrative content.

## 3. Interface elements that ARE content models

Each element below earns its place only if manipulating it *is* making a tense-aspect decision. Mapping to the three decisions from prior research (reference point / viewpoint / verb kind) plus contrast:

| Interface element | Exemplar | What the learner physically does | Content distinction it *is* | Decision it embodies |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Matrix timeline with glyph grammar** | Lingolia Timeline *(verified live: clicked Progressive)* | Clicks a row (aspect) or column (time); the matrix filters; glyph in each cell changes shape | Simple = ✕ point marks; Progressive = wavy line crossing the vertical reference line; Perfect = earlier mark *linked* to the reference line; same verb (*bake a cake*) in all 12 cells, only the glyph + auxiliary change | Viewpoint (row) × reference point (column). Holding the verb constant makes the *switches* the only variable — the interface performs "one switch at a time" |
| **Summary chart as home screen** | Azar Ch.1 chart + Chartbook | Reads across/down a printed grid; returns to it from any unit (pop-up in Azar Interactive) | Time × aspect grid with timeline drawing per cell | The whole decision space, permanently visible; a chart the learner can *point at* while composing |
| **Live sentence builder** | IIENSTITU Tense Explorer *(verified live)* | Picks subject / verb / object / adverb from slots; the tool instantly assembles positive + negative + question for the chosen tense; a **Compare tab** puts two tenses side-by-side | Form is an *output* of slot choices, never a starting text; adverbs are a slot, so learners see *since/for/yesterday* doing anchoring work | Form composed last, mechanically: choose meaning-bearing slots → read off the form |
| **Contrast split-screen** | Lingolia comparison page *(verified live prior turn)*; British Council two-column tables; IIENSTITU Compare | Reads one dialogue that *switches* (perfect opens: *Have you ever been to Norway?* → past simple details: *I went there in 2014*), then a two-column use-table, then signal-word lists | The pair is one screen with one decision (finished vs unfinished time; specific *when* vs no *when*) | Contrast pair as a *single* unit interface — after the two singles exist (Murphy prints the same logic: singles in units 7–8, the pair as its own units 13–14) |
| **Adverb-deleted drill (structured input)** | VanPatten processing-instruction activities (research) | Hears a sentence and must choose a NOW or PAST picture; **the time adverb has been removed from the interface**, so only the verb ending carries the information | The drill interface *deletes the redundant cue* Chinese learners over-rely on (temporal adverbials — Li & Choe 2017 found exactly this reliance, plus present perfect used with definite adverbials) | Reference-point anchoring must be read off the verb, not the adverb — the interface refuses to let the learner bypass the form |
| **Story player with viewpoint controls** | Duolingo Stories; BBC *The Race* drama + narrative-tense units; narrative-tense interactives | Plays a story line by line; answers checks *inside* the story; in narrative interactives, taps tense buttons to highlight which sentences are main events / background / flashback in the same scene | Past simple = plot moves; past continuous = background already playing; past perfect = flashback — one scene, three layers, colour/toggle separated | Viewpoint as *camera layer*: the same event re-rendered by toggling the layer is the lesson |
| **Mastery-coloured review surface** | Busuu Review tab (colour by strength); Duolingo progress ring + Legendary unit replay; Babbel Review Manager | Re-meets items the system has scheduled; sees item colour darken/fade with retention | Mastery is a *state of each cell/item*, decaying over time, not a station marked "done" | Spiralling made visible: the home surface itself shows what is fading |
| **Struck-through error element** | Perfect English Grammar *NOT: ~~I've seen him yesterday~~*; Murphy correct-the-sentence exercise; Azar editing checklist | Reads/produces the *wrong* form, visibly cancelled, beside the repair | The anticipated Chinese-learner error (*\*I have seen him yesterday*, *\*I am here since…*, perfect = 了) is a first-class screen object, not a footnote | Error anticipation inside the unit interface |

**The glyph grammar is the shared cross-product language.** Independent timeline traditions converge on the same four marks: **✕/point** = bounded event, **wavy/extended line** = ongoing at the reference point, **arrow/bridge from an earlier ✕ to the reference line** = perfect ("earlier than, and connected to, the reference point" — *not* "completed": Lingolia's own perfect definition, verified live, is "view a completed action as *connected to a later time*… the *result* is emphasised"), **? at a line's end** = unknown start/finish. Any tense widget whose marks don't follow this grammar (e.g. a "time machine" that only relabels the destination) is inventing a private language the learner must learn *in addition to* English.

## 4. Ranked interface exemplars for verb/tense teaching

Ranked by one criterion: *how much of the content structure is carried by the interface itself* (manipulable model > visible model > good zoning > mere ordering).

1. **Lingolia Timeline + tense pages (as one system).** The only free product where the home object (matrix), the unit page (dialogue→uses→form) and the contrast pages share one visual language, and where clicking the model changes the model. Weaknesses: exercises sit behind links; the timeline is a destination, not a persistent widget inside units; no mastery state, no route.
2. **Azar (print + Interactive).** Best *static* interface: system-before-parts contents order, warm-up before chart, chart as a returnable home object (even sold alone as the Chartbook), pop-up charts inside digital practice. Weakness: the chart is read-only; the learner never moves its switches.
3. **Murphy.** Best *page* interface: the spread makes presentation and practice co-visible, situations force forms, margin arrows network the units, the Study Guide is an honest diagnostic router for a deliberately non-linear book. Weakness: as a whole-system map it is only a list; relations live inside units, not on the contents screen.
4. **British Council LearnEnglish grammar pages.** Best *cheap* interface pattern: test→explain→retest on one page, contrast as a two-column table whose headers are the distinction. Weakness: no system map; pages are findable by level shelves, not by relation.
5. **IIENSTITU Tense Explorer.** Best *form-composition* element (slot builder + compare tab + per-tense mini-timelines on one dashboard), but it is a reference tool, not a course: no route, no progression, no story, formula-first card labels (*S + have/has + V3* is the card's subtitle — form leads).
6. **Duolingo.** Best *progress* interface (path, ring, in-path stories/review, difficulty tiers — validated against the tree by its own research) and the worst *content* interface: the tense system has no screen. Copy its routing and review mechanics; do not copy its invisibility of grammar.
7. **Busuu / Babbel.** Middle: CEFR shelves + linear feed (unremarkable), redeemed by two elements worth stealing — Busuu's mastery-coloured Review surface and Mistake Repair, Babbel's grammar callouts anchored to a dialogue already heard.
8. **BBC Learning English.** Best *story shell* (serial drama, topic units, pinned grammar-reference box), but tense is buried inside topics with no tense-indexed home — a cautionary example of narrative swallowing the curriculum map.
9. **Perfect English Grammar.** The cautionary baseline: MAKE/USE split across URLs, exercises as outbound links. Excellent *content*, lecture-station interface — i.e. roughly the app's current design with better prose.

## 5. The interface grammar the best products share

1. **One constant example across the whole system.** Azar's chart, Lingolia's *bake a cake* in all 12 cells: when only the switch changes, the switch is what is seen.
2. **Rows and columns are decisions, not topics.** Time is one axis, viewpoint the other; a "tense" is an address in the grid, never a free-standing chapter.
3. **Meaning is encountered before form is displayed** — in scroll order (Lingolia), in page order (Murphy's scene before rule; Azar's warm-up before chart), or in programme order (Babbel's dialogue before its callouts). Form-first interfaces (formula as a card title) exist only in reference tools.
4. **Practice shares a surface with presentation** — facing page, same page (British Council), pop-up chart inside the exercise (Azar Interactive). Every "click away to practise" measurably turns a unit into reading.
5. **A contrast is a split screen, not a second lecture.** Two columns, one dialogue that switches, one compare tab — and it is scheduled *after* both singles (Murphy 7–8 → 13–14; English File File 4 → File 6 revisits the same pair *for/since*-first vs *yet/just/already*-first).
6. **The first screen of a strong unit is an attempt or a scene, never a definition.** (Azar warm-up; British Council pre-test; Lingolia/Babbel dialogue; Murphy pictured situation.) Definition-first is the lecture-station signature.
7. **Mastery is rendered as state on the map, not as completion of stations** — colour/strength per item (Busuu), a ring that can be re-earned at a harder tier (Duolingo Legendary), review nodes inserted into the route (Duolingo path, per DRR-24-04).
8. **Errors are screen objects.** Struck-through forms, correct-the-sentence slots, editing checklists and personal Mistake Repair lists put the *predicted* wrong form on the same surface as the right one.
9. **Every unit interface has a "where this lives" element** — margin arrows (Murphy), left-rail topic tree with the current tense highlighted (Lingolia), a pinned reference box (BBC), a guidebook tied to the unit banner (Duolingo). A unit that doesn't show its own address in the system teaches an orphan fact.

## 6. What the 18 lecture stations should become — an interface specification

The current interface — *a vertical list of 18 stations; each station = a preset on a time-machine widget + an explanation text; exercises mostly missing* — fails on all three interface questions: the list shows neither progress nor system; the station screen is definition-first and read-only except for a preset; the widget's manipulation (choosing a destination tense) does not correspond to any decision the learner must learn to make. Redesign, in the exemplars' grammar:

### 6.1 Home screen: replace the list with a *matrix with a route on it*
- The home screen **is** the time-machine, drawn as Lingolia/Azar's grid: columns = reference point (past / now / future), rows = viewpoint (whole event ✕ / inside the event ∼ / looking back from the reference point ⌒), with the glyph grammar of §3. One constant character + verb (the app's mascot, one verb) lives in every cell.
- The **recommended route is threaded through the grid** as a Duolingo-style path drawn *on* the matrix (present simple & continuous first, past simple, the future pair, present perfect, its contrasts, past continuous in stories, past perfect last) — so sequence and system are the *same picture*: the learner sees both "what's next" and "what this is next *to*". This is the one move none of the exemplars makes (Duolingo has route without system; Lingolia/Azar have system without route) and it is the app's opportunity to be better than all of them.
- Each cell/node carries a **mastery colour** (Busuu) that fades with time; faded cells spawn review nodes on the route (Duolingo DRR-24-04's in-path review). Contrast pairs are drawn as **bridges between two cells**, unlocked only when both endpoint cells are lit (Murphy's singles→pair order, made spatial).
- The 18 topics stop being 18 stations: several become *properties of the home screen itself* (overview/two switches = the grid's axes tutorial; verb-form system = the builder, §6.2; adverbial anchoring = a slot in every unit; Chinese 了 transfer = an error surface, §6.3). What remains as visitable units is ~12 cells + 4–5 bridges.

### 6.2 Unit screen: one surface, five zones, in this order
Copied zone-by-zone from the dissected units (§2), with the manipulable element in zone 2 doing the teaching:
1. **Scene first (Murphy/Babbel/Lingolia):** a short story/dialogue panel — playable, line by line — in which the target form is doing its job; target forms are tappable, not pre-highlighted into passivity.
2. **The decision widget (the unit's core; replaces the preset):** the learner *re-renders the same scene* by moving the actual switches — drag the reference point along the timeline; flip viewpoint (stand outside the event ✕ / step inside it ∼ / step to the reference point and look back ⌒); swap the verb card (bounded verb *break/lose/arrive* vs activity *play/rain* vs state *know/live* — the Aspect-Hypothesis order: teach past/perfect first on bounded verbs, progressive first on activities, and *show* states refusing the progressive). The sentence under the scene **re-composes itself as the switches move**; the form (auxiliaries, *-ed/-ing/participle*) is assembled in view, last — the IIENSTITU builder's logic, but driven by meaning switches instead of formula slots.
3. **The chart strip (Azar):** a one-screen summary chart for this cell *and its neighbours* (the cell lit, neighbours dimmed), pinned so it remains visible — or one tap away — during practice (BBC's pinned reference box; Azar Interactive's pop-up).
4. **Practice on the same surface, in the exemplars' gradient:** (a) *structured-input* items with the time adverb **deleted** — hear/read and place the event on the timeline; the verb ending is the only cue (VanPatten); (b) *scene→sentence* items (Murphy's situation format); (c) *find-and-fix* items built from Chinese-learner errors (§6.3); (d) *personalisation* (write about your own timeline — Murphy's last exercise; Busuu-style corrected production); (e) a *story retelling* in which the same picture sequence must be retold after the widget forces a viewpoint shift (background it / flash it back).
5. **Retest gate (British Council):** the unit opens with a 3-item attempt (Azar warm-up) and closes with the same *kind* of items; the delta, not the lecture, is what marks the cell's colour on the home grid.

### 6.3 The pair bridges and the 了 surface
- **Pairs are bridges, one split screen:** left half / right half of *one* scene (living vs dead person's experiences for perfect vs past; a timetable card vs a decision made now for timetable-present vs *will*; clouds gathering vs *I think…* for *going to* vs *will* evidence; a duration bar measured from a point = *since* vs measured as a length = *for*). The learner's job on a bridge is always the same physical act: **drag each new sentence to the half whose reference point it uses** — classification by manipulation, then production.
- **Chinese 了 gets an error surface, not a lecture:** a dedicated bridge where 了-sentences are dragged onto the grid and *split* — the same 了 lands in past simple (bounded, finished time named), present perfect (result visible now) or nowhere near perfect (state verbs) — making visible that 了 is a boundedness mark, not a tense (the transfer pattern documented in Li & Choe 2017 and the *le/guo/zhe* review: *le*→perfective, *guo*→experience-perfect, *zhe*→durative-progressive are three different bridges, not one translation).

### 6.4 Interface traps — shapes that look natural and teach badly
1. **The list of stations** (current design): implies tenses are 18 independent facts; no relations, no route logic, no fading. A list is a contents page pretending to be a curriculum.
2. **The path without a map** (Duolingo's failure mode): perfect motivation, zero system; the learner can finish the route unable to say how any two forms relate, and cannot *find* a form again (no search, no address).
3. **The preset slider / time machine that only relabels:** if dragging changes the tense *name* and the paragraph but the scene, glyph and sentence don't re-render, the widget teaches "tense = label", the exact misconception the three-decision model exists to kill.
4. **The chart as poster:** Azar's chart works because units *route through it* (warm-up → chart → work, pop-ups in practice). Pasted as a decorative overview screen that is never returned to, a matrix is wallpaper.
5. **MAKE and USE on different screens** (Perfect English Grammar): splitting form and meaning across pages/tabs guarantees many learners meet only one of them.
6. **Practice behind a door:** exercises on a separate page/tab/app section (Lingolia's weakness, PEG's failure) — every click between presentation and practice leaks learners.
7. **Topic-hidden grammar** (BBC): stories are the best *unit* shell and the worst *home* screen; if the home screen is topics, the tense curriculum has no address and cannot be reviewed by form.
8. **Completion ticks instead of decaying state:** a station marked "done" forever contradicts everything spaced-review interfaces (Busuu colour, Duolingo ring/review nodes) encode — that mastery is a colour that fades.
