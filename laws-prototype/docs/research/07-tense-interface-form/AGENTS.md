> Research question: How do the world's best textbooks, courses and software STRUCTURE AND TEACH the English tense-aspect curriculum as a sequence of teachable units — unit by unit? A Chinese children's English app currently presents its 18 tense-aspect topics (TA units) as a list of lecture stations: each station = a preset on a tense 'time machine' widget + an explanation text, with exercises mostly missing. We already know from prior research that the best model is three manipulable decisions (reference point / viewpoint / verb kind) with form composed last. This new research must NOT repeat that; it answers the CURRICULUM and UNIT-DESIGN questions:
> 
> 1. SEQUENCE: In what order do the best syllabi introduce the tense-aspect topics, and why? Compare: Azar's Understanding and Using English Grammar chapter order; Murphy's English Grammar in Use (units 1–~50 order: present continuous → present simple → past simple → past continuous → present perfect → …); Cambridge/Oxford coursebooks; CEFR-graded progressions (A1→B1 tense inventory per level, e.g. from the English Grammar Profile / EVP — which tense forms are expected at A1, A2, B1); app paths (Duolingo, Busuu, Lingoda, Babbel — where tenses appear in their course trees); Chinese national-curriculum textbook order （人教版/外研版 PEP: when 一般过去时/现在进行时/一般将来时/现在完成时 first appear by grade). Extract the consensus sequence and the notable disagreements (e.g. present perfect timing, past continuous before/after perfect, future forms order).
> 
> 2. UNIT ANATOMY: For one tense topic, how is a complete teaching unit built in the best materials? Examine concrete units: a Murphy unit (left-page presentation/right-page exercises anatomy), an Azar chart+exercise unit, a Perfect English Grammar topic page, a Lingolia tense page (explanation + interactive timeline + graded exercises), a BBC Learning English session (e.g. Lower Intermediate Unit on present perfect), one 洋葱学园 or Chinese app tense lesson if publicly describable. For each: the presentation medium (dialogue? story? timeline? contrast pair?), whether the meaning or the form comes first, the practice gradient (recognition → controlled → guided production → free production), how errors are anticipated inside the unit, and how the unit connects back/forward (spiral revisiting).
> 
> 3. CONTRAST PAIRS as units: several of the hardest topics are really PAIRS (past simple vs present perfect; past simple vs past continuous; will vs going to; present simple vs present continuous; since vs for). How do the best materials teach the pairs — single combined unit or separate-then-contrast? Evidence from materials design and any classroom studies on contrastive presentation timing.
> 
> 4. EXERCISES: what exercise types do the best units actually contain per tense topic (beyond gap-fill): timeline labelling, picture-sequence narration, find-the-mistake with learner-realistic errors, personalisation prompts, story retelling with forced viewpoint shifts, translation traps for Chinese learners. Name concrete exercise sets (Murphy's exercise formats, Azar's, EnglishPage's, Lingolia's) and any research on exercise-type effectiveness for tense (e.g. processing instruction — VanPatten's structured input studies on tense/aspect forms).
> 
> 5. ASSESSMENT/MASTERY: how do courses decide a learner has 'got' a tense (mastery criteria in apps: Duolingo/Busuu spaced repetition of tense items; Lingolia progress; textbook review units), and how are tenses spiralled back in later units?
>
