# Whiteboard Animation for Teaching — How the Best Software and Video Teaches English Prepositions

## Summary

**The one-sentence answer:** A good whiteboard lesson is not "pictures appearing on a white background." It is **drawing order = thinking order**: a scene is built stroke-by-stroke, in sync with narration, so the learner predicts, watches one relationship change, and names the new meaning — and then the *same* scene is kept on screen and altered (not wiped) for the next sense. The research isolates exactly two load-bearing ingredients: **(1) progressive drawing** (vs. showing the finished picture) and **(2) a narrative / concrete scene to anchor it**. A visible hand helps mainly as an attention-pointer and social cue — a moving cursor/pen-tip matched it in one controlled study — while most of what makes commercial whiteboard videos look "cheap" (stock images sliding in undrawn, walls of typed text, robotic constant-speed pacing, default hand/font/library) contributes nothing to learning and is explicitly warned against by VideoScribe's own makers.

**Confidence:** High for the learning-science claims (multiple peer-reviewed, controlled experiments, cited below, with honest null results included). Medium for frame-by-frame exemplar descriptions: YouTube was blocked in the live research browser by a Google "unusual traffic" bot-check on 2026-11-01 (not solved, per policy), so video descriptions rest on publisher lesson pages, full chapter/timestamp lists, and timestamped transcripts obtained via the web index — flagged `index` — not on a live frame-by-frame viewing. Everything that *was* opened and read is flagged `verified live` (browser_open).

**Best model to copy for a preposition app:** Kevin's "in = a space, on = a surface, at = a point" lesson (one core image-schema, story first, then the *same* schema re-mapped from place to time) fused with Tyler & Evans' proto-scene method (one trajector–landmark diagram, one change at a time, senses radiating out) and RSA Animate's large-canvas accumulation (never erase what the learner still needs to compare against).

## Findings

### 1. Whiteboard-animation explainer exemplars in language teaching

#### 1.1 engVid — the "real teacher at a real whiteboard" pole (NOT animation)

**engVid — James: "English Grammar: The Prepositions ON, AT, IN, BY"**
URL: https://www.engvid.com/english-grammar-the-prepositions-on-at-in-by/ — `verified live`, read 2026-11-01. Embedded video: `https://www.youtube.com/embed/sN5H7YTo_IQ` (from the page).

- What the viewer sees: a standing teacher in front of a physical whiteboard, marker in hand, talking to camera. The board is filled *by hand during the lesson* — headings, example sentences, underlinings — in the order he speaks them. There is no drawn scene/picture grammar at all: the visual channel is handwritten *words and lists*, organised by use-case (transport → location → time).
- Content structure (from the lesson and its indexed summary): opens with a confusion hook — "on a taxi or in a taxi? in a plane or by plane?" — then sorts: ON = large/public vehicles you can stand on (bus, train, plane, boat) and surfaces; IN = enclosed/private (car, taxi, room, box); AT = specific point/location, no transport sense; BY = how you travel / proximity. Ends with a recap table across transport/location/time and a linked quiz on engVid (the page itself hosts "Test your understanding of this English lesson").
- Narration/personality: direct, humorous, teacher persona *is* the engagement device. Gaze alternates board ↔ camera (Mayer's gaze-guidance, §3).
- What it does best: **trust, personality, and worked contrast in handwriting** — learners see the teacher *decide* and write, errors/corrections included; zero production barrier.
- Limitations for copying: no visual scene for the spatial meaning itself — "in = enclosed" is *stated*, never *drawn as a container with something inside it*. A child/ESL beginner gets a rule list to memorise, precisely what proto-scene teaching (§4) tries to avoid. Ronnie's lessons are the same format: e.g. **"PREPOSITIONS in English: under, below, beneath, underneath"**, https://www.engvid.com/prepositions-in-english-under-below-beneath-underneath/ — `index`, read 2026-11-01, embedded video `https://www.youtube.com/embed/7jIqZjfx_uM` — teacher at board distinguishing near-synonyms verbally; and **Jon, "Prepositions in English – on time or in time?"**, https://www.engvid.com/prepositions-in-on-time/ — `index`.

#### 1.2 Woodward English — the "clean animated chart" pole (typed/drawn-slide hybrid)

**Woodward English — "AT ON IN – Prepositions of Time in English"**
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Lr1BBoNu6hI — `index` only (YouTube blocked live), read 2026-11-01. The full chapter list in the description is the evidence for pacing:

> 0:00 Introduction · 0:19 AT + Specific Time · 2:00 AT + Holiday Period · 2:35 ON + Days · 4:12 ON + Dates · 5:20 IN + Months · 7:24 IN + Years · 8:10 IN + the Decade · … 10:52 IN + Time Period · 13:20 ON + Day + Part of the Day · 14:08 AT the weekend or ON the weekend? · 14:49 AT + the moment — plus "summary charts" at the end (description).

- What the viewer sees (from this and the companion lesson pages): crisp, flat, colourful *animated slides in a hand-friendly rounded style* — not a hand drawing in real time. Each use gets a header, a set of example sentences appearing one by one, and small spot illustrations. Segments are long (AT-time ≈ 100 s; IN-months ≈ 2 min) and exhaustive.
- **Woodward — "Basic Prepositions of Place"**, lesson page https://www.woodwardenglish.com/lesson/basic-prepositions-of-place/ (reached via https://www.woodwardenglish.com/search/PREPOSITIONS+OF+PLACE/feed/rss2/) — `verified live`, read 2026-11-01. This is the clearest "one landmark, move the trajector" treatment in the set:
  - One fixed landmark (a box; in the chart, a postman/dogs/fire-hydrant cast) and one moving trajector (a ball / a dog).
  - Sentence-by-sentence accumulation: "The ball is **behind** the box / **in front of** the box / **next to** the box / **between** the boxes / **near** the box / **above** the box / **below** the box / **in** the box / **on** the box."
  - A second, funnier chart re-instantiates the *same* relations with dogs ("The dog is **on** the table", "There is a thermometer **in** the dog's mouth") — same schema, new cast = a recap that is also a joke.
- What it does best: **exhaustive, predictable, classroom-safe coverage + summary charts**; the ball-and-box is exactly the TR/LM device of §4, made child-friendly. Teachers can buy the same lesson as a silent PowerPoint (description) — proof the visual sequence carries meaning even without narration.
- Limitations: (a) it *lists* relations, it never *transforms* one into another — the ball teleports between finished tableaux, so the learner never sees "above → drop into contact → on" as a change; (b) it actively conflates neighbours the mission cares about: the page states "**above = over**" and "**below = under**" as synonyms — linguistically wrong for the covering/trajectory senses of *over* (§4); (c) ~15 min for in/on/at-time is far beyond the 2.5-min scribe norm (§2) — it survives on chaptering, not brevity.

#### 1.3 The strongest single preposition lesson found: Kevin 英文不難 — "不用死背，一次搞懂 IN, ON, AT" (Chinese-language, core-schema)

URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cNJhNfyHAtg — `index`, with a **full timestamped transcript**, read 2026-11-01. This is the only lesson in the set that teaches a *core scene and then its extension*, which is the mission's §(d) problem. Beat by beat from the transcript:

| Time | What happens |
|---|---|
| 0:00–0:18 | Hook in Chinese: in/on/at "look rule-less, so you memorised them — but one core concept makes it simple." Promise of *one* rule. |
| 0:22–0:28 | **The core schema, stated as three pictures:** "in 是空間範圍 (a spatial extent), on 是一個接觸的表面 (a surface in contact), at 就是一個點 (a point)." |
| 0:39–1:58 | **A story first, unexplained:** a dialogue (Anna/Brian arrange dinner) that naturally contains *in Taipei, on Xinyi Road, at the restaurant, at 7 p.m., on Friday*. Learner hears the senses *in use* before any analysis. |
| 2:10–2:40 | **Replay with freeze-and-explain:** the same dialogue is re-walked; each preposition is stopped on and mapped back to the schema — Taipei is a space you're inside → *in*; a road is a surface you stand on → *on*; the restaurant as a meeting *point* → *at*. |
| ~3:08–3:30 | **Extension to time — same drawing, new domain:** "概念差不多" — *in* = a stretch of time (in 10 minutes, in winter, in 2025), *on* = a specific date/day (on Friday, on May 8th), *at* = a precise time-point (at 7 p.m., at noon). Place-schema and time-schema are explicitly declared to be *the same concept*. |

- What it does best: **proto-scene teaching without the jargon** — dimensionality (3-D container / 2-D surface / 0-D point) does all the work, and the place→time extension is shown as *re-labelling the same picture*, not as a new rule list. Narration is bilingual-scaffolded (English examples, Chinese explanation), paced as story → replay → generalisation.
- Limitations: it is a talking-head / screen lesson, not literally hand-drawn stroke-by-stroke; the schema is mostly verbal + simple graphics, so the *drawing* grammar must be borrowed from RSA/Khan (§1.5–1.6). It also stops at in/on/at; it does not do a trajectory word like *over*.

A compact English cousin of the same trick: **BBC Learning English (Sam), via 新东方**, https://mtoutiao.xdf.cn/www/bbc/yingyu/202108/11213237.html — `index`, read 2026-11-01: *at* = a single specific location ("I'm at work / at the BBC"), *on* = a surface ("sitting on a chair"), *in* = a 3-D space "similar to inside" ("in a room"), memorised through **one single scene**: "I'm **at** the front. The band is **on** the stage. And my friends are **in** the crowd." One concert picture, three prepositions — the cheapest possible side-by-side contrast (§2).
