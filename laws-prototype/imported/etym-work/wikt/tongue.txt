===Etymology===
From {{inh|en|enm|tongue}}, a late spelling of {{m|enm|tonge|tong(e)|id=tongue}}, {{m|enm|tunge|tung(e)}}, from {{inh|en|ang|tunge}}, from {{inh|en|gmw-pro|*tungā||tongue}}, from {{inh|en|gem-pro|*tungǭ||tongue}}, from {{inh|en|ine-pro|*dn̥ǵʰwéh₂s||tongue}}. {{doublet|en|langue|lingua}}.

{{col-top|2|Notes}}
* The expected modern spelling, both phonetically and etymologically, would be {{m|en|tung}}. Using ⟨on⟩ for ⟨un⟩ was fairly common in {{m+|enm|-}}; compare e.g. {{m|enm|yong||young}}. The final ⟨gue⟩ arose to prevent {{m|enm||tonge}} being misread with a [[soft g|soft]] {{IPAchar|/dʒ/}}. However, this spelling only became common at a time when the final ⟨e⟩ was already largely silent, so it is not clear why it was not simply dropped instead.<ref>Oxford University Press. (n.d.). Tongue, n., Etymology. In Oxford English dictionary. Retrieved June 27, 2025, from https://doi.org/10.1093/OED/2664387729</ref> Perhaps the spelling was influenced directly by {{cog|frm|langue||tongue}}.
* {{senseno|en|language|cap=1}} (“a language”) is an ancient [[metonym]], already in use more than one thousand years ago in Old English,<br/>going back all the way to Proto-Germanic (the oldest-possible form of English and [[Germanic|other tongues]]), and common across Indo-European languages ([[cf.]] {{cog|hy|լեզու}}, {{cog|ga|teanga}}, {{cog|la|lingua}}, {{cog|fa|زبان|tr=zabān}} and {{cog|ru|язык}}). Its persistent modern usage, which prevented it from becoming fully {{lg|archaic}} or {{lg|obsolete}} in favor of the [[Gallicism]] {{m|en|language}}, is likely due to its natural metonymicity and to steady historical reinforcement by {{cog|xno|langue}} (which had both meanings too) and by, to a lesser extent, native poetic imitation of {{cog|la|lingua}} during the [[Renaissance]].
