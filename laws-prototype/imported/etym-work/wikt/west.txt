#* {{RQ:Spenser Faerie Queene|passage=Foure times his place he shifted hath in sight, / And twice has risen, where he now doth '''West''', / And '''wested''' twice, where he ought rise aright.|5|prologue}}
#* {{quote-book |en |year=1997 |last=Kurlansky |first=Mark |authorlink=Mark Kurlansky |chapter=Chapter 4: 1620: The Rock and the Cod |title=Cod: A Biography of the Fish that Changed the World |publisher=Walker |isbn=9781440672873 |passage=The ships would leave [[Europe]] in [[April#English:_Q118|April]], when there was a good [[easterly#Adjective|easterly]] wind. [[trans-Atlantic#Adjective|Trans-Atlantic]] mariners wanted the wind [[dead#Adverb|dead]] on the [[stern#Noun|stern]], so they could run with it, sticking to the same [[latitude]]—a method called easting and westing. [[w:Christopher Columbus|Columbus]], [[w:John Cabot|Cabot]], and many others had done this. Until the eighteenth century, there was no reliable way to measure [[longitude]], which helps explain why so many could make [[landfall|landfalls]] from the [[Bahamas]] to [[Newfoundland]] and believe they were near [[Asia]]. Latitude could be fixed by the relationship to the [[North Star]] or [[sun#Proper noun|the sun]], one of the few things known in the sixteenth century about celestial [[navigation]]. '''Westing''' ships out of [[Bristol#English:_Q23154|Bristol]] reached the [[Labrador#English:_Q380307|Labrador]] coast and dropped down to Newfoundland. [[Breton|Bretons]] went straight to the [[Grand Bank]] and [[St. John's#English:_Q2082|St. John's]]. If a ship left the harbor at [[La Rochelle#English:_Q82185|La Rochelle]], dropping below the two islands that protect that harbor, and then stuck to its latitude, it would make landfall at [[Cape Breton]], where the French station of [[Louisbourg#English:_Q3263622|Louisbourg]] was established so that La Rochelle ships could easily find it.}}
