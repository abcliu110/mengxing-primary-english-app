===Etymology 1===
{{ety|en|:inh|enm:peple}}
From {{inh|en|enm|puple}}, {{m|enm|peple}}, {{m|enm|peeple}}, from {{derived|en|xno|people}}, from {{derived|en|fro|pueple}}, {{m|fro|peuple}}, {{m|fro|pople}}, from {{derived|en|la|populus|t=a people, nation}}, from {{der|en|itc-ola|populus}}, from earlier {{m|itc-ola|poplus}}, from even earlier {{m|itc-ola|poplos}}, from {{der|en|itc-pro|*poplos|t=army}} of unknown origin. {{doublet|en|pueblo}}. Gradually ousted native {{cog|en|lede}} and, partially, {{m|en|folk}}.

Originally used with singular verbs (e.g. "the people is hungry, and weary, and thirsty, in the wilderness" in the King James Version of 2 Samuel 17:29<ref>{{R:King James Version|2 Samuel|17|29|page=426|column=2|text={{...}}The people ''is'' hungrie, and wearie, and thirſtie in the wilderneſſe.|nodot=1}}</ref>), the plural aspect of ''people'' is probably due to influence from {{cog|enm|lede}}, {{m|enm|leed}}, a plural since [[w:Old English|Old English]] times; see {{m|ang|lēode}}.

=====Usage notes=====
* The word ''people'' today takes a plural verb in most senses.
* Nowadays, ''persons'' as the plural of ''person'' is considered highly formal. Several major style guides recommend ''people'' rather than ''persons''. For example, the Associated Press and the New York Times recommend ''people'' except in quotations and set phrases. Under the traditional distinction, which {{w|Garner's Modern English Usage|Garner}} says is pedantic,<ref>{{R:Garner's|edition=4th|page=682|entry=people}}</ref> ''persons'' describes a finite, known number of individuals, rather than the collective term ''people''. ''Persons'' remains in use in technical and legal contexts.

===Etymology===
Since 2000, named after ''{{w|People (magazine)|People}}'', an American weekly magazine that specializes in celebrity news, human-interest stories, and gossip. That word itself is derived from {{der|fr|fro|peuple}}, thus making it a reborrowing. {{doublet|fr|peuple|pueblo}}.
