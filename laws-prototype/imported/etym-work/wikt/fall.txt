Noun from {{inh|en|enm|fal}}, {{m|enm|fall}}, {{m|enm|falle}}, from {{inh|en|ang|feall}}, {{m|ang|ġefeall|t=a falling, fall}} and {{inh|en|ang|fealle|t=trap, snare}}, from {{inh|en|gem-pro|*fallą}}, {{m|gem-pro|*fallaz|t=a fall, trap}}. Cognate with {{cog|yol|vall|t=fall}}, {{cog|nl|val|t=fall}}, {{cog|de|Fall|t=fall}}, {{cog|da|fald|t=fall}}, {{cog|fo,is,nb,nn,sv|fall|t=fall}}.
{{col-bottom}}

Sense of "autumn" is attested by the 1660s in [[England]] as a shortening of ''[[fall of the leaf]]'' (1540s), from the falling of leaves during this season. Along with ''autumn'', it mostly replaced the older name ''[[harvest]]'' as that name began to be associated strictly with the act of harvesting. Compare ''[[spring]]'', which began as a shortening of “spring of the leaf”.

=====Usage notes=====
* The phrase {{m|en|have a fall}}, as opposed to {{m|en|fall over}} or {{m|en|fall down}}, is typically reserved for older people for whom a fall is more likely to be a medical emergency. However, this phrase can be considered patronizing by those to whom it is applied.<ref>{{RQ:Guardian|url=https://www.theguardian.com/commentisfree/2022/jun/14/young-fall-over-older-people-stepmother-broke-knees|title=The young fall over, older people ‘have a fall’ – and my stepmother is none too happy about it|author=Zoe Williams|date=14 June 2022|archiveurl=https://web.archive.org/web/20261018094109/https://www.theguardian.com/commentisfree/2022/jun/14/young-fall-over-older-people-stepmother-broke-knees|archivedate=18 October 2026|ascite=1}}</ref><ref>{{cite-web|titleurl=https://medium.com/crows-feet/are-we-talking-about-falls-the-wrong-way-9a41d9b3b410|title=Are We Talking About Falls The Wrong Way?|first=Janice|last=Harayada|date=12 November 2023|work=Crow's Feet|archiveurl=https://web.archive.org/web/20260201020829/https://medium.com/crows-feet/are-we-talking-about-falls-the-wrong-way-9a41d9b3b410|archivedate=1 February 2026}}</ref>
