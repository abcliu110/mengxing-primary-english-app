=====Usage notes=====
* In colloquial speech, the particle {{m|fr|ne}} is commonly omitted (see the entry). Hereby the difference between the senses “more” and “not any more” becomes blurred. So in the above example, {{m|fr||Elle travaille plus}} could mean both “She works more” and “She doesn't work any more”. In practice, however, this distinction is maintained because the pronunciation of {{m|fr||plus}} is different in each sentence: {{m|fr||Elle travaille plus}} {{IPAchar|/ɛl tʁavaj '''plys'''/}} means “more”, and {{m|fr||Elle (ne) travaille plus}} {{IPAchar|/ɛl (nə) tʁavaj '''ply'''/}} means “not any more”.

====Usage notes====
* In classical Latin, always plural when used as an adjective. The neuter singular {{m|la|plūs}}, inflected from the same stem, is used only as a pronoun or adverb. To express "more" of a singular noun denoting an uncountable substance, the pronoun {{m|la|plūs}} is used with the genitive singular of the noun: e.g. {{m|la||plūs}} {{m|la|aquae}} "more water", literally "more of water", {{m|la||plūs}} {{m|la|āeris}} "more air", literally "more of air".
* The word {{m|la|maior|t=greater}}, the comparative of {{m|la|magnus}}, is used to express greater magnitude, and is sometimes used in contexts where English might use "more" (e.g. {{m|la|maior}} {{m|la|pecūnia}} "more money; a greater sum",<ref>{{cite-book|page=210|pageurl=https://books.google.com/books?id=cBIZp5LwQCMC&pg=PA210|title=Latin Grammar Together with a Systematic Treatment of Latin Composition|last=Fischer|first=Gustavus|year=1897}}</ref> {{m|la|maiōre}} {{m|la|vī}} "with more/greater force", {{m|la|maiōre}} {{m|la|spatiō}} {{m|la|temporis}} "more time; a greater interval of time").
