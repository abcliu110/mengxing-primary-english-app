===Etymology===
{{root|en|ine-pro|*ǵʰéysd-}}
The {{lg|noun}} is derived from {{inh|en|enm|gost}}, from {{inh|en|ang|gāst}}, {{m|ang|gǣst|t=breath, spirit, soul, ghost}} (compare modern {{cog|en|Holy Ghost}}), from {{inh|en|gmw-pro|*gaist}}, from {{inh|en|gem-pro|*gaistaz}}, from {{inh|en|ine-pro|*ǵʰéysdos}}, from {{m|ine-pro|*ǵʰéysd-|t=anger, agitation}}.<ref name="OED">Compare {{R:OED Online|pos=n.'' and ''adj|id=6509148342|date=September 2025|nodot=1}}; {{R:Lexico|pos=n}}</ref>

The ''h'' in the spelling appears in the Prologue to {{w|William Caxton}}’s ''Royal Book'', printed in 1484, in a reference to the “[[Holy Ghost|Holy Ghoost]]”. It was likely influenced by {{cog|dum|gheest}}, a common variant of {{m|dum|geest}}. Both Caxton and his assistant {{w|Wynkyn de Worde}} had connections to the Low Countries.<ref name="OED"/><ref>{{cite-book|author=w:David Crystal|title=Spell It Out: The Singular Story of English Spelling|location=London|publisher=Profile Books|year=2012|isbn=978-1-84668-567-5}}</ref> {{doublet|en|geist}}.

The {{lg|adjective}} and {{lg|verb}}<ref>{{R:OED Online|pos=v|id=1999598935|date=March 2025|nodot=1}}; {{R:Lexico|pos=v}}</ref> are derived from the noun. The verb gained prominence in the 2010s.<ref>{{cite-web |en |url=https://www.npr.org/2025/10/22/nx-s1-5580542/ghost-etymology-halloween-spooky-season |work=Word of the Week |publisher={{w|NPR}} |accessdate=2025-10-23 |date=2025-10-22 |title=The not-so-spooky origins of 'ghost'&nbsp;— and why the word still haunts our language |first=Joe |last=Hernandez|archiveurl=https://web.archive.org/web/20251128145554/https://www.npr.org/2025/10/22/nx-s1-5580542/ghost-etymology-halloween-spooky-season|archivedate=28 November 2025}}</ref>
