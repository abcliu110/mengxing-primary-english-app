===Etymology===
{{ety|en|:lbor|la:metamorphōsis|tree=1}}
{{lbor|en|la|metamorphōsis}}, from {{der|en|grc|μεταμόρφωσις|t=transformation}}, from {{m|grc|μεταμορφόω|t=to transform, disguise}} + {{m|grc|-σῐς|pos={{glossary|suffix}} forming {{glossary|abstract noun}}s or {{glossary|noun}}s of action, process, or result}}; the former is from {{m|grc|μετᾰ-|pos={{glossary|prefix}} forming terms concerning change}} + {{m|grc|μορφόω|t=to shape, form}}.<ref name="OED">{{R:OED Online|pos=n|id=7374725284}}</ref><ref name="Etymonline">{{R:Online Etymology Dictionary|pos=n}}</ref> The prefix is from the {{glossary|preposition}} {{m|grc|μετᾰ́|t=of sequence or succession; after|ng=; of uncertain origin}}, whereas the {{glossary|verb}} is from {{m|grc|μορφή|t=shape, form|ng=; also of uncertain origin}} + {{m|grc|-όω|pos=suffix forming verbs with a {{glossary|causative}} or {{glossary|factitive}} meaning}}. Analyzable as {{af|en|meta-<t:later, subsequent>|-morph<t:shape, form, structure>|-osis<t:process, action>}}.

First attested in 1533 in the English politician, author, and philosopher {{w|Thomas More}}’s work ''The Debellacyon of Salem and Bizance'': {{l|en|metamorphosis|see the quotation|id=transformation}}.<ref name="OED"/><ref name="Etymonline"/> Compare {{cog|enm|Methamorphoseos,Methamorphosios,methamorphosyos|t=the poem ''{{w|Metamorphoses}}'' by the Roman poet {{w|Ovid}} or its contents}}, also attested in 1531 in the English politician and writer {{w|Thomas Elyot}}’s work ''{{w|The Book of the Governor}}'',<ref>{{RQ:Elyot Governour|ascite=1|chapter=What Ordre Shulde Be in Lernynge&nbsp;/ and Whiche Autours Shulde Be Fyrst Redde|folio=34|passage=I wolde ſet nexte vnto hym two bokes of Ouid&nbsp;/ the one called ''Metamoꝛphoſios''&nbsp;/ whiche is as moche to ſay&nbsp;/ as chaungynge of men in to other figure oꝛ fourme: {{...}}}}</ref> from the {{glossary|singular}} {{glossary|genitive}} form of the Ancient Greek term, {{m|grc|μετᾰμορφώσεως}}.<ref name="OED"/><ref>{{R:MED Online|entry=Metamorphōseōs|pos=n|id=MED27536}}</ref>

The use of {{senseno|en|botany}} in [[botany]] was influenced by the German writer and polymath {{w|Johann Wolfgang von Goethe}}’s work ''{{lang|de|Versuch die Metamorphose der Pflanzen zu erklären}}'', known in English as ''{{w|Metamorphosis of Plants}}'' (1790).<ref name="OED"/> The specific definition of {{senseno|en|chemical reaction}} in [[chemistry]] is a {{semantic loan|en|de|Metamorphose|nocap=1}}, this name having been applied in 1842 by the German chemist {{w|Justus von Liebig}} in his work ''Die Thierchemie oder die organische Chemie in ihrer Anwendung auf Physiologie und Pathologie'', with an English translation by [[w:William Gregory (chemist)|William Gregory]] published the same year as ''Animal Chemistry; or, Organic Chemistry in Its Applications to Physiology and Pathology''.<ref>{{cite-journal|editor=&#91;{{w|John Gibson Lockhart}}&#93;|month=June|year=1842|title=Art{{quote-gloss|icle}} IV.—''Animal Chemistry; or the Application of Organic Chemistry to the Elucidation of Physiology and Pathology''. By Justus Liebig, M.D. Edited from the German MS. by William Gregory,{{nb...|M.D., Professor of Chemistry, King’s College, Aberdeen. 8vo. London, 1842.}}|journal=[[w:Quarterly Review|The Quarterly Review]]|volume=LXX|issue=CXXXIX|location=London|publisher=[[w:John Murray (publishing house)|John Murray]],{{nb...|Albemarle Street.}}|page=99|pageurl=https://archive.org/details/sim_quarterly-review-1809_june-september-1842_70_139-140/page/99/mode/1up|oclc=1009026207|passage=Professor Liebig, then, applies the name of metamorphosis to those chemical actions in which a given compound, by the presence of a peculiar substance, is made to resolve itself into two or more new compounds: as, for example, when sugar, by the presence of ferment or yest, is made to yield alcohol and carbonic acid.}}</ref>
