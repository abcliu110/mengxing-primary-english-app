===Etymology===
{{root|en|ine-pro|*pnew-}}
From {{inh|en|enm|snesen|t=to sneeze}}, alteration of earlier {{m|enm|fnesen|t=to sneeze}}, from {{inh|en|ang|fnēosan|t=to sneeze, snort}}, from {{inh|en|gmw-pro|*fneusan}}, from {{inh|en|gem-pro|*fneusaną}}, from {{der|en|ine-pro|*pnew-|t=to breathe, pant, snort, sneeze}}. Cognate with dialectal {{cog|nl|fniezen|t=to sneeze}}, {{cog|non|fnýsa|t=to snort}}.

Compare {{m|en|neeze}}, from {{cog|enm|nesen}}, from {{cog|ang|*hnēosan|t=to sneeze}}, cognate with {{cog|goh|niosan|t=to sneeze}}, {{cog|non|hnjósa|t=to sneeze}}. See {{m|en|neeze}}.

It has been suggested that the change could be due to a misinterpretation of the uncommon initial sequence ''fn-'' as ''ſn-'' (''sn-'' written with a [[long s]]),<ref>{{R:Lexico}}</ref><ref>{{R:Etymonline}}</ref><ref>[https://wordhistories.net/2017/06/02/origin-of-sneeze/ word histories: sneeze]</ref> although the change is regular, seen also in {{m|en|snore}} and {{m|en|snort}} from {{cog|enm|fnoren}} and {{m|enm|fnorten}}, and in late {{cog|enm|snatted}} from earlier {{cog|enm|fnatted||snub-nosed}}. The ''fn-'' forms of all these words fell out of use in the 1400s. Due to this rather universal adoption of the ''fn-'' > ''sn-'' shift within English by such a time frame, the idea of it being a simple sound shift has been suggested as well, with it specifically being a type of [[w:Assimilation (phonology)|assimilation]] as the labial ''f-'' becomes alveolar ''s-'' to match the place of articulation of the following ''n-''.
