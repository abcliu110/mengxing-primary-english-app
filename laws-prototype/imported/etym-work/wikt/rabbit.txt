===Etymology 1===
{{inh+|en|enm|rabet}}, {{m|enm|rabette}}, from Anglo-{{der|en|la|rabettus}}, from dialectal {{der|en|fro|rabotte}}, probably a diminutive of {{der|en|dum|-}} or {{der|en|vls|robbe|t=rabbit, seal}}, of {{unc|en|nocap=1}} origin; possibly some {{onomatopoeic|en|title=imitative}} verb, maybe {{m|dum|robben}}, {{m|gml|rubben|t=to rub}} is used here to allude to a characteristic of the animal. See {{m|en|rub}}.

Related forms include {{cog|frm|rabouillet|t=baby rabbit}} and in {{cog|fr|rabot|t=plane}}), coming via {{cog|wa|-}} {{der|en|fro|-}} (reflected nowadays as {{cog|wa|robète|t=rabbit}}), from {{der|en|dum|robbe}} {{gl|"rabbit; seal"; whence Modern {{cog|nl|rob|t=rabbit", also "seal}}}}; also {{cog|gml|robbe}}, {{m|gml|rubbe|t=rabbit}}, and the later {{cog|nds-de|Rubbe}}, {{m|nds-de|Robb|t=seal}}, {{cog|fy|robbe|t=seal}}, {{cog|stq|Rubbe|t=seal}}, {{cog|frr|rob|t=seal}}, borrowed into {{cog|de|Robbe|t=seal}}.

Meant "young rabbit" until the 19th c., when it came to replace the original general term {{m|en|cony}}, owing to the latter's resemblance to and use as a euphemism for {{m|en|cunny}}, "vulva" (compare {{m|en|ass}} and {{m|en|donkey}}).

Note that there is no inherited Germanic word for rabbits, since hares are the only [[leporids]] native to Britain (as with all of Europe outside the {{w|Iberian Peninsula}} and southwest France); rabbits were introduced from France in the late Middle Ages, likely after the {{w|Norman Invasion}}. (Fittingly, {{m|en|hare}} is indeed inherited from Proto-Germanic.)
