====Usage notes====
Some usage guides seek to distinguish “jealous” from “envious”, using ''jealous'' to mean “protective of one’s ''own'' position or possessions” – one “jealously ''guards'' what one has” – and ''envious'' to mean “desirous of ''others’'' position or possessions” – one “''envies'' what others have”.<ref>“[https://web.archive.org/web/20091005191428/http://www.wsu.edu/~brians/errors/envious.html Envious/Jealous]”, Paul Brians, ''[https://web.archive.org/web/20100208172743/http://www.wsu.edu/~brians/errors/book.html Common Errors in English Usage]''</ref> This distinction is also maintained in the psychological and philosophical literature.<ref>See [[w:Jealousy#Comparison with envy|Jealousy: Comparison with envy]] and [[w:Envy#Envy.2C_jealousy_and_schadenfreude|Envy: Envy, jealousy and schadenfreude]]</ref><ref>[http://plato.stanford.edu/ Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy], [http://plato.stanford.edu/entries/envy/ Envy], [http://plato.stanford.edu/entries/envy/#1.2 1.2 Envy vs. Jealousy]</ref> In common usage, however, although ''envious'' is always with respect to others’ possessions or fortune, ''jealous'' does not always refer strictly to one’s own possessions (as shown by the citations above).
