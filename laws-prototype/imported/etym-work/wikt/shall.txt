====Usage notes====
* ''Shall'' is generally about one-fourth as common as ''[[will]]'' in North America compared to in the United Kingdom. Lack of exposure leads many in North America to consider it formal or even [[pompous]] or [[archaic]], best reserved for court decisions and legal contracts. North Americans mainly use it in senses two and three.
** However, this effect itself is in flux and perhaps fading due to factors like the [[Americanization]] of the non-US [[Anglosphere]] (especially in younger generations), and the will/shall distinction seems to be flattening in colloquial use into the latter carrying a mild tinge of poetic emphasis.
* In law, ''shall'' is typically used to impose obligation, though the word can also convey discretionary power or recommendation. Due to its ambiguity, some jurisdictions refrain from using the term in law drafting and official writing.<ref>{{cite-book|year=2012|author=Law Drafting Division, Department of Justice|title=Drafting Legislation in Hong Kong — A Guide to Styles and Practices|url=https://www.elegislation.gov.hk/b0000000022.attach|page=90|passage=LDD no longer uses “shall” to impose an obligation or its negative forms to impose a prohibition. Moreover, it is not used for any other purpose for which it had been used.}}</ref><ref>{{cite-book|year=2011|author=Plain Language Action and Information Network|title=Federal Plain Language Guidelines|url=https://www.plainlanguage.gov/media/FederalPLGuidelines.pdf|page=25|passage=Besides being outdated, “shall” is imprecise. It can indicate either an obligation or a prediction. Dropping “shall” is a major step in making your document more user-friendly.}}</ref><ref>{{cite-book|year=2012|author=New Zealand Law Commission|title=Legislation Manual: Structure and Style|url=https://www.lawcom.govt.nz/sites/default/files/projectAvailableFormats/NZLC%20R35.pdf|page=43|passage=Although shall is used to impose a duty or a prohibition, it is also used to indicate the future tense. This can lead to confusion. Shall is less and less in common usage, partly because it is difficult to use correctly.}}</ref><ref>{{cite-book|year=2012|author=Office of Parliamentary Counsel, Australia|title=Plain English Manual|url=https://www.opc.gov.au/sites/default/files/2023-01/plain_english_0_0.pdf|page=20|passage=The traditional style uses “shall” for the imperative. However, the word is ambiguous, as it can also be used to make a statement about the future. Moreover, in common usage it’s not understood as imposing an obligation.}}</ref>
* In the past, ''will'' and ''shall'' were interchangeable and synonymous, used similarly as auxiliary verbs for the future tense but separate persons. The simple future tense traditionally used ''shall'' for the first person ("I" and "we"), and '''will''' for the second and third persons. This distinction existed largely in formal language and gradually disappeared in [[Early Modern English]].
*: ''I '''shall''' go.''
*: ''You '''will''' go.''
** An emphatic future tense, indicating volition of the speaker—determination, promise, obligation, or permission, depending on the context—, reverses the two words, using ''will'' for the first person and ''shall'' for the second and third person.
**: ''I '''will''' go.''
**: ''You '''shall''' go.''
** Usage can be reversed in questions and in dependent clauses&mdash;especially with indirect discourse. For example: '''''Shall''' you do it?'' anticipates the response ''I '''shall''' do it.'' Or: ''he says that he '''shall''' win'' or ''he expects that he '''shall''' win'' anticipate his saying ''I '''shall''' win'', not ''I '''will''' win.''
* The second-person singular form (used with {{m|en|thou}}) is irregular, {{m|en|shalt}}. {{m|en|shallest|Shallest}}, {{m|en|shalst}} are rare, often hypercorrect.
* The past tense form ''should'' can be used in reported speech, e.g., ''I hoped that I '''should''' find you here.'' This use of ''should'' may not be readily apparent to speakers who do not often use ''shall''.
