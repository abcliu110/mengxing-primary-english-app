====Usage notes====
* The bare form '''{{m|de||all}}''' is used with articles and pronouns, which it precedes (as in English). For instance: {{m|de||all die Sachen|all the things}}; {{m|de||all dies[es] Gerede|all this chitchat}}; {{m|de||all[e] meine Freunde|all my friends}} {{q|more common with the e}}. Colloquial German often uses the adjective {{m|de|ganz}} instead: {{m|de||die ganzen Sachen; dies[es] ganze Gerede; meine ganzen Freunde}}.
* If '''{{m|de||all}}''' is followed by an adjective, the adjective is declined weakly: {{m|de||alle guten Sachen|all good things}}, {{m|de||alles Gute|all the best}}

====Usage notes====
{{m|nb|all|All}} agrees with the following noun: ''all'' with masculine and feminine singular nouns, ''alt'' with neuter singular nouns, and ''alle'' with plural nouns. In its ordinary quantitative use, the singular refers to a mass or quantity, while the plural refers to countable individuals: ''all malinga'' (“all the paint”), ''alt vannet'' (“all the water”) and ''alle bøkene'' (“all the books”). To refer to one complete countable object, {{m|nb|hel|hele}} is normally used instead: ''hele boka'' (“the whole book”).<ref>{{cite-web|title=17 Grammatikk|work=LearnNoW Youth|publisher=Norwegian University of Science and Technology|url=https://www.ntnu.edu/learnnowyouth/17/grammar|nodate=yes|accessdate=2026-10-26}}</ref>

Used independently, {{m|nb|alt}} can mean “everything”, while {{m|nb|alle}} can refer to everyone or to all the members of an understood group. Older quotations retain the spelling ''al''.
