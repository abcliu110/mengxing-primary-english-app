===Etymology 1===
{{ety|en|id=drink|:bor|nl:thee}}
First appears c. 1655, in the English translation {{w|Álvaro Semedo}}’s writings. From {{bor|en|nl|thee}}, from {{der|en|nan-hbl|茶|tr=tê}} ({{w|Amoy dialect}}), from {{der|en|och|-}}, ultimately from {{der|en|sit-pro|*s-la||leaf, tea}}. Introduced to English and other Western European languages by the {{w|Dutch East India Company}}, who sourced their tea in {{w|Amoy}}; compare {{cog|ms|teh}} along the same trade route. {{doublet|en|chai|cha}} (and, distantly, the first element of {{m|en|lahpet}}), from same Proto-Sino-Tibetan root; see discussion of cognates.

{{box-top|Cognates}}
The word for “tea” in many languages is of Sinitic origin (due to China being the origin of the plant), and thus there are many cognates; see [[#Translations|translations]]. These are from one of two proximate sources, reflected in the phonological shape: forms with a stop (e.g. {{IPAchar|/t/}}) are derived from Min Nan ''tê'', while forms with an affricate (e.g. {{IPAchar|/tʃ/}}) are derived from other Sinitic languages, like Mandarin ''chá'' or Cantonese ''caa4'' (all written as {{zh-l|*茶}}). Different languages borrowed one or the other form (specific language and point in time varied), reflecting trade ties, generally Min Nan ''tê'' if by ocean trade from Fujian, Cantonese ''caa4'' if by ocean trade from Guangdong, or northern Chinese ''chá'' if by overland trade or by ocean trade from India.<ref>''The World Atlas of Language Structures Online,'' “[http://wals.info/feature/138 Chapter 138: Tea]”, by Östen Dahl</ref>

Thus Western and Northern European languages borrowed ''tê'' (with the exception of Portuguese, which uses {{m|pt|chá}}; despite being by ocean trade, their source was in {{w|Macao}}, not Amoy), while ''chá'' borrowings are used over a very large geographical area of Eurasia and Africa: Southern and Eastern Europe, and on through Turkish, Arabic, North and East Africa, Persian, Central Asian, and Indic languages. In Europe the ''tê''/''chá'' line is Italian/Slovene, Hungarian/Romanian, German/Czech, Polish/Ukrainian, Baltics/Russian, Finnish/Karelian, Northern Sami/Inari Sami. ''tê'' was also borrowed in European trade stops in Southern India and coastal Africa, though ''chá'' borrowings are otherwise more prevalent in these regions, via Arabic or Indic, due to earlier trade. The situation in Southeast Asia is complex due to multiple influences, and some languages borrowed both forms, such as Malay {{m|ms|teh}} and {{m|ms|ca}}.
