=====Usage notes=====
* In its senses of “enjoy” and “maintain as a regular habit”, {{m|en||like}} is a [[catenative verb]]; in the former, it usually takes a [[gerund]] ({{m|en|-ing}} form), while in the latter, it takes a {{m|en|to}}-[[infinitive]]. ''See also'' [[Appendix:English catenative verbs]].
* ''Like'' is only used to mean “want” in certain expressions, such as ''[[if you like]]'' and ''[[would like]]''. The conditional form, {{m|en|would like}}, is used quite freely as a polite synonym for {{m|en|want}}.

=====Usage notes=====
* ''{{w|The American Heritage Dictionary}}'' opines that using ''like'' as a conjunction, instead of ''as'', ''the way'', ''as if'', or ''as though'', is strictly [[Appendix:Glossary#informal|informal]]; it has, however, been routine since the [[Middle English]] period. ''AHD4'' says, "Writers since Chaucer's time have used ''like'' as a conjunction, but 19th-century and 20th-century critics have been so vehement in their condemnations of this usage that a writer who uses the construction in formal style risks being accused of illiteracy or worse", and recommends using ''as'' in formal speech and writing. ''{{w|The Oxford English Dictionary}}'' does not tag it as colloquial or [[Appendix:Glossary#nonstandard|nonstandard]], but notes, "Used as conj[unction]: = 'like as', 'as'. Now generally condemned as vulgar or slovenly, though examples may be found in many recent writers of standing."

=====Usage notes=====
The use as a quotative is informal; it is commonly used by young people, and commonly disliked by older generations, especially in repeated use. It may be combined with the use of the present tense as a narrative. (For its use preceded by a form of ''be'', see ''[[be like]]''.) Similar terms are ''to go'' and ''all'', as in ''I go, “Why did you do that?” and he goes, “I don't know”'' and ''I was all, “Why did you do that?” and he was all, “I don't know.”'' These expressions can imply that the attributed remark which follows is representative rather than necessarily an exact quotation; however, in speech these structures do tend to require mimicking the original speaker's inflection in a way {{m|en|said}} would not.
