===Etymology===
{{ety|en|:inh|enm:sche}}
{{inh+|en|enm|sche}}, {{m|enm|scho}}, {{m|enm|hyo}}, {{m|enm|ȝho|t=she}}, whence also Yorkshire dialectal {{m|en|shoo|t=she}}, {{cog|sco|she}}, {{m|sco|sho|t=she}}.

Probably from {{inh|en|ang|hēo}}<ref name="CHEL">{{cite-book|en|year=1992|title=Cambridge History of the English Language|chapter=Phonology and Morphology|volume=II|editor=Norman Blake|author=Roger Lass|pages=118-119}}</ref><ref name="OED">{{R:OED Online|she|pos=''pron.¹, n., and adj.''|id=177685|noformat=1|date=September 2013}}</ref> (whence dialectal {{cog|en|hoo}}), with an irregular change in stress from ''hēo'' to ''heō'' {{IPAchar|/hjoː/}}, then a development from {{IPAchar|/hj-/}} to {{IPAchar|/ç/}} to {{IPAchar|/ʃ-/}},<ref name="Fulk"/><ref name="Clark"/> similar to the derivation of ''Shetland'' from {{m+|non|Hjaltland}}. In this case, ''she'' is from {{inh|en|gmw-pro|*hiju}}, from {{inh|en|gem-pro|*hijō||this, this one|g=f}}, from {{der|en|ine-pro|*ḱe-}}, {{m|ine-pro|*ḱey-||this, here}}, and is cognate with {{cog|stq|jo}}, {{m|stq|ju}}, {{cog|fy|hja}}, {{cog|frr|jü}}, {{cog|da|hun}}, {{cog|sv|hon}}; more at {{m|en|he}}.

A derivation from {{cog|ang|sēo|t=that one”, occasionally “she}} is also possible, though less likely.<ref name="OED"/><ref name="Fulk">{{cite-book|author=R. D. Fulk|title=An Introduction to Middle English: Grammar and Texts|year=2012|pages=64-65}}</ref><ref name="Clark">Cecily Clark, ''The Peterborough Chronicle, 1070-1154'' (1970), page lxvi-lxvii: "Most favoured recently has been derivation from ''heo''/''hie'', though a chain of development [hi] > [hj] > [ç] > [ʃ]. The decisive change [ç] > [ʃ] has been various explained, either as an example of a Norse sound-change found elsewhere only in a few place-names (the so-called "Shetland theory") or as substitution of a common initial phoneme for a rare one, linked with the need to maintain the distinction both from masculine ''he'' and from second-person plural ''ȝe''. Geographical distribution of''h-'' and ''sch-'' forms during the Middle English period is said to support derivation from stress-shifted [hjè]. [... There] still seems, however, something to be said for the older theory put forward in ''NED'': that ''scæ'' developed from ''sie'', through [sjè]. Certainly the demonstrative ''seo'' was used as an emphatic pronoun, as, for instance, in ''Sermo in Festis S. Marie''; and, although the variant nom. sing. ''sie'' is regularly used only in the ''Vespasian Psalter Gloss'' (once in ''Rushworth St. Matthew''), such forms as ''sy ea'' in the E Preface (beside DF ''seo'') and ''si [...]'' in the ''Northhamptonshire Geld-Roll'' suggest that it may have been current in Peterborough usage as well. This theory explains better than that of derivation from stress-shifted ''heo''/''hie'' the coexistence of ''sch-'' and ''h-''forms in the same text, as in ''Sir Gawain'' (''scho'' and ''ho'') and ''William of Palerne'' (''sche'' and ''he''). Its weakness lies in failing to explain why the [ʃ], regular in the pronoun, never occurs in the demonstrative [...]"</ref> In that case, {{m|ang|sēo}} would have undergone a change in stress from ''sēo'' to ''seō'' {{IPAchar|/sjoː/}}, then a change from {{IPAchar|/sj-/}} to {{IPAchar|/ʃ-/}}, similar to the derivation of ''sure'' from {{m+|fro||seur}}.<ref name="Clark"/><ref name="Wardale">E. E. Wardale, ''An Introduction to Middle English'' (1937 [2016]), chapter VI (pp. 91-92 and notes), covers other proposed explanations, including that it is from a mixture of both {{m|ang|hēo}} and {{m|ang|sēo}}</ref> It would then be cognate to {{cog|nl|zij}} and {{cog|de|sie}}.

Neither etymology would be expected to yield the modern [[vocalism]] in {{IPAchar|/iː/}} (the expected form would be {{m|en|shoo|id=she}}, which is in fact found dialectally). It may be due to influence from {{m|en|he}},<ref name="CHEL"/> but both {{m|ang|hēo}} and {{m|ang|sēo}} also have rare variants ({{m|ang|hīe}} and {{m|ang|sīe}}) that may give modern English {{IPAchar|/iː/}}.<ref name="Clark"/><ref>{{R:ang:Dictionary of Old English|hē, hēo, hit|E17157}}</ref>

====Usage notes====
* Since at least the 1920s and 30s, some [[gay]] or [[queer]] men refer to other gay or queer men and/or themselves with ''she''/''her'' pronouns, as well as with other feminine terms such as ''[[Miss]]'' and ''[[girl]]'', to signal their sexuality rather than their<!-- sex or--> gender identity;<ref>Greville G. Corbett, ''The Expression of Gender'' (2013), page 26: "There are uses of she to refer to people who are attributed and claim male sex. Rudes and Healy 1979 give many examples collected in their ethnolinguistic investigation among gay males in Buffalo, NY."</ref> this has sometimes been termed "the [[gay she]]":<ref>Kirby Conrod, "Pronouns in motion", in ''Lavender Linguistics'' (2018), page 11</ref><ref>Anna T., ''[http://cup.columbia.edu/book/opacity-minority-improvisation/9783837651331 Opacity - Minority - Improvisation]: An Exploration of the Closet Through Queer Slangs and Postcolonial Theory'' ({{ISBN|9783837651331}}, 2020), pages 84-85</ref>

* {{quote-book|en|year=1997|author=Anna Livia; Kira Hall|title=Queerly Phrased: Language, Gender, and Sexuality|page=359|passage=In English, gay men often use the female pronoun ''she'' to refer to other gay men:<br>SPEAKER A: Speaking of fags, where is Miss Thing?<br>SPEAKER B: You mean Ron?<br>SPEAKER A: Yeah.<br>SPEAKER B: I don't know where she is.<br>(Rudes and Healy ['Is She For Real'] 1979: 61)<br>{{...|In this example, Ron is first introduced as Miss Thing, a feminine designator that is then anaphorized as ''she'' from grammatical necessity There are also cases where no antecedent feminine is introduced to justify the use of feminine pronouns, [...] In this case, ''she'' is deictic rather than anaphoric and therefore stands out as more marked. [...] This use of female pronouns to refer to gay men is limited to specifically gay contexts; it can have dire social consequences if the device misfires. As such, it is a statement of sexual orientation rather than of sexual [/gender] identity. The men who use these feminine forms to refer to themselves or to other gay men are designating themselves, as well as the referents, as traitors to heterosexual masculinity.}}

===Etymology===

===Particle===
{{head|gv|particle|dependent form|nee}}

# {{non-gloss|Present/future copula form}}
#: {{ux|gv|'''She''' ynseyder eh Juan.|John is a teacher.|inline=1}} {{qualifier|definition: predicate is indefinite}}
#: {{ux|gv|'''She''' Juan yn ynseyder.|John is the teacher.|inline=1}} {{qualifier|identification: predicate is definite}}
#: {{ux|gv|'''She''' mish honnick eh.|It's me who saw him.|inline=1}} {{qualifier|cleft sentence}}
#: {{ux|gv|'''She''' Juan ta ny ynseyder.|It's John who is a teacher.|inline=1}} {{qualifier|cleft sentence}}

====Usage notes====
Used in present and future sentences for identification or definition of a subject as the person/object identified in the predicate of the sentence. Used to introduce [[w:cleft sentence|cleft sentences]], which are extremely common in Manx. It is not a verb. For the particle that introduces adjectives, see {{m|gv|s'}}.

''She'' has no past tense; the appropriate conjugation of {{m|gv|ve}} must be used instead.
