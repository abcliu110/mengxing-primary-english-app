=====Usage notes=====
* In some American dialects around Maine and New England, especially in children's speech, this word can be used positively,<ref>[https://ygdp.yale.edu/phenomena/so-dont-i So Don't I]</ref> most particularly in the construction ''So don't I'' in response to a proud statement by the previous speaker.
* The use of ''don't'' with the sense of ''does not'' seems to be attested first in the 17th century, a time when the use of do as an auxiliary in negative clauses ([[w:Do-support|''Do''-support]]) was increasing. Its development may be related to the omission of the suffix -s on the verbs {{m|en|need}} and {{m|en|dare}} when used as auxiliaries in the negative. Another likely analogy was the rhyming word {{m|en|won't}}. Although now nonstandard, it seems not to have been felt as incorrect by some authors of the 18th and 19th centuries.<ref>Joly, André (1990), "He don't / Don't he ? in the History of English : a Study in Psychosemiology", in Roux, Louis (director, CIEREC), ''L'Organisation du sens, domaine anglais'': recueil en l'honneur de Jean Lavédrine, pages 125-137.</ref> By the second half of the 19th century, however, some writers on grammar were prescribing the use of ''[[doesn't]]'' rather than ''don't'' in this context.<ref>{{cite-web|title=Was ‘it don’t’ once good English?|site=Grammarphobia|url=https://www.grammarphobia.com/blog/2023/06/dont.html|year=2023|author1=Patricia T. O’Conner|author2=Stewart Kellerman|archiveurl=https://web.archive.org/web/20260517124216/https://grammarphobia.com/blog/2023/06/dont.html|archivedate=17 May 2026}}</ref> Even today, {{m|en||he don't}} tends to be more acceptable in colloquial speech than {{m|en||he say, he make}}, etc., which are clearly marked as dialectal. "Don't" is often used in place of "doesn't" in songs for meter effect, even by singers who wouldn't ordinarily do so in speech.
