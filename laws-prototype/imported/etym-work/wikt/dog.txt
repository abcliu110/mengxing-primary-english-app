===Etymology 1===
{{wikidata lexeme|L1122}}
{{etymon|en|id=canine|:inh|enm:dogge|tree=1}}
{{inh+|en|enm|dogge}}<ref>Hans Kurath, Sherman M. Kuhn, ''Middle English Dictionary'' (1962, {{ISBN|0472010441}}), page 4, page 1204</ref> (akin to {{cog|sco|dug}}), from {{inh|en|ang|docga,*dogga}},<ref>{{R:ang:BT|docga}}</ref><ref>Laurel Brinton, Alexander Bergs, ''Old English'' (2017, {{ISBN|3110525305}}), page 59: "In addition, the velar [ɡɡ] and palatal [ɡɡj] geminates could be written as <gg> or <cg>, as in <dogga> ~ <docga> ..."; Richard M. Hogg, Norman Francis Blake, ''The Cambridge History of the English Language'' (1992, {{ISBN|052126474X}}), volume 1, age 91 says much the same.</ref> of {{unc|en|nocap=1}} origin.<ref name="npr">{{cite-web |en |url=https://www.npr.org/2025/08/13/nx-s1-5500476/dog-history-etymology-origin |title=Why the origin of the word 'dog' remains a mystery |work={{w|All Things Considered}} |date=2025-08-13 |accessdate=2025-08-18 |first=Juliana |last=Kim |publisher={{w|NPR}}|archiveurl=https://web.archive.org/web/20260920144330/https://www.npr.org/2025/08/13/nx-s1-5500476/dog-history-etymology-origin|archivedate=20 September 2026}}</ref>

The original meaning seems to have been a common dog, as opposed to a well-bred one, or something like '[[cur]]', and perhaps later came to be used for stocky dogs. Possibly a pet-form diminutive with suffix {{m|ang|-ga}} (compare {{m|ang|frocga|t=frog}}, {{m|ang|*picga|t=pig}}), appended to a base {{m|ang|*dog-}}, {{m|ang|*doc-}} of unclear origin and meaning. One possibility is {{der|en|ang|dox|t=dark, swarthy}} (compare {{m|ang|frocga}} from {{m|ang|frox}}).<ref>Piotr Gąsiorowski, 2006. [https://repozytorium.amu.edu.pl/jspui/bitstream/10593/2479/1/Dogga.pdf The Etymology of Old English *docga]. ''Indogermanische Forschungen'', 111.</ref> Another proposal is that it derives from {{cog|gmw-pro|*dugan||to be suitable}}, the origin of {{cog|ang|dugan||to be good, worthy, useful}}, {{cog|en|dow}}, {{cog|nl|deugen}}, {{cog|de|taugen}}. The theory goes that it could have been an epithet for dogs, commonly used by children, meaning "good/useful animal".<ref>[https://www.oed.com/view/Entry/56405]</ref> Another one is that it is related to {{m|ang|*docce|t=stock, muscle}}, from {{m+|gmw-pro|*dokkā|id=bundle|t=round mass, ball, muscle, doll}}, whence {{m+|en|dock|id=cut|t=stumpy tail}}.

In 14th-century England, {{m|en|hound}} (from {{m+|ang|hund}}) was the general word for all domestic canines, and ''dog'' referred to a subtype resembling the modern mastiff and bulldog.<ref name="dog">[http://hrcak.srce.hr/index.php?show=clanak&id_clanak_jezik=48370&lang=en]</ref> By the 16th century, ''dog'' had become the general word, and ''hound'' had begun to refer only to breeds used for [[hunt]]ing.<ref>[https://books.google.com/?id=uZcs6poXkfEC&pg=PA215]</ref> In the 16th century, the word ''dog'' was adopted by several continental European languages as their word for {{m|en|mastiff}}.<ref>{{R:Online Etymology Dictionary|dog}}</ref>

Despite similarities in forms and meaning, it is not related to {{noncog|vmb|dog}}.
