===Etymology 1===
The sense of “burden” first arose in the 13th century as a secondary meaning of {{inh|en|enm|lode}}, {{m|enm|loade}}, which had the main significance of “way, course, journey”, from {{inh|en|ang|lād||course, journey; way, street, waterway; leading, carrying; maintenance, support}} (ultimately from {{inh|en|gem-pro|*laidō||leading, way}}, {{der|en|ine-pro|*leyt-||to go, go forth, die}}.

Cognate with {{cog|gml|leide||entourage, escort}}, {{cog|de|Leite||line, course, load}}, {{cog|sv|led||way, trail, line}}, {{cog|is|leið||way, course, route}}. As such, {{m|en||load}} is a {{doublet|en|lode|nocap=1}}, which has preserved the older meaning.

Most likely, the semantic extension of the Middle English substantive arose by conflation with the (etymologically unrelated) verb {{m|en|lade}}; however, Middle English {{m|en||lode}} occurs only as a substantive; the transitive verb {{m|en||load|t=to charge with a load}} is recorded only in the 16th century (frequently in Shakespeare),<ref>
Walter W. Skeat, ''An Etymological Dictionary of the English Language'' (2013), [https://books.google.com/books?id=jeeGAAAAQBAJ&pg=PA345 p. 345].</ref>
and (except for the participle {{m|en|laden}}) has largely supplanted {{m|en|lade}} in modern English.<ref>"but lade is now usually replaced in the present and the past tense by load, a derivative from the noun load". Hans Kurath, George Oliver Curme, ''A grammar of the English language'' vol. 2 (1935), p. 262.<!--
"lade" surives into the 20th century in technical (largely nautical) usage, but in everyday contexts appears to be essentially supplanted by load already by the end of the 18th century.
C.f. the biblical usage in Gen. 42:26: KJV (1611, retained in 1769) "laded", Thomson (1808) "loaded", Webster's Revision of the KJV (1833): "loaded".-->
</ref>

For the meaning development from PIE, compare {{m+|la|carrus}} (whence {{m|en|carry}}) akin to {{m|la|currō}}.
