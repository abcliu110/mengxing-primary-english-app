Origin and history of love love (n.) Middle English love , from Old English lufu "feeling of love; romantic sexual attraction; affection; friendliness; the love of God; Love as an abstraction or personification." This is reconstructed to be from Proto-Germanic *lubo, from PIE root *leubh- "to care, desire, love." The weakened sense "liking, fondness" is attested in Old English. The meaning "a beloved person" is by early 13c. The sense of "no score" (in tennis, etc.) is by 1742, from the notion of playing for love (1670s), that is, for no stakes. Phrase for love or money "for anything" is attested by 1580s. The phrase no love lost (between two people) is ca. 1600 (Ben Jonson). It is originally used in senses of one individual loving more than the other, such that love is lost (i.e. unrequited). It could be used 17c. in reference to two who love each other well, as well as two who have no liking for each other (1620s, the usual modern sense). To fall in love is attested from early 15c.; to be in love with (someone) is from c. 1500. To make love is from 1570s in the sense "pay amorous attention to;" as a euphemism for "have sex," it is attested from c. 1950. Love affair "a particular experience of love" is from 1590s. Love life "one's collective amorous activities" is from 1919, originally a term in psychological jargon. Love beads is from 1968. Love bug , imaginary insect, is from 1883. Middle English loven , from Old English lufian "to feel love for, cherish, show love to; delight in, approve," from Proto-Germanic *lubojanan (source also of Old High German lubon , German lieben ), a verb from the root of love (n.). The weakened sense of "like, be very pleased or satisfied with" is attested by c. 1200. The intransitive sense of "be in love, have a passionate attachment" is from mid-13c. To pluck flower petals with he loves me ... he loves me not is from the Gretchen Tragedy in "Faustus" (in the English translations by 1821), Er liebt mich—liebt mich nicht , whence Shelley got it ("The serpent is shut out from Paradise"). To love (someone) up "make out with" is from 1921. To love and leave is from 1885.
