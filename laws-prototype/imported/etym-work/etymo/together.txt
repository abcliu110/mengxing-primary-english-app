Origin and history of together together (adv.) "in company, in conjunction, simultaneously," Middle English togeder , from Old English togædere "so as to be present in one place, in a group, in an accumulated mass," from to (see to ) + gædere "together" (adv.), which is apparently a variant of the adverb geador "together," from Proto-Germanic *gaduri- "in a body" (from PIE *ghedh- "to unite, join, fit;" see good , and compare gather ). In reference to single things, "so as to be unified or integrated," from c. 1300. German equivalent zusammen has as second element the Old High German verbal cognate of English same (Old English also had tosamne "together"). Formerly also used without semantic content as a literal representation of Latin con- , com- when that element had lost meaning or become merely intensive in the Latin word, as þenke to gedre for commemoro or Wycliffe's "þei sholn beten togidere [Latin conflabunt ] þeir swordis in to shares & þer speris in to siþes" [Isaiah ii:4]. Togethers , with adverbial genitive, was used through 16c. together (adj.) "self-assured, free of emotional difficulties or hang-ups," by 1966, from together (adv.). also from 1966
