Origin and history of were were (v.) indicative plural and subjunctive singular and plural of was , Old English wæron (past plural indicative of wesan ) and wære (second person singular past indicative); see was . The forms illustrate Verner's Law (named for Danish linguist Karl Verner, 1875), which predicts the "s" to "z" sound shift, and rhotacism, which changed "z" to "r." Wast (second person singular) was formed 1500s on analogy of be/beest, displacing were. An intermediate form, wert, was used in literature 17c.-18c., before were reclaimed the job. were (n.) Middle English wer , "a man, husband, male person, masculine member of a pair of sexually differentiated species;" from wer "man, male person" (from PIE root *wi-ro- "man"). Obsolete except in compounds.
