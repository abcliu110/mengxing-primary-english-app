Origin and history of twin twin (adj.) Old English twinn "consisting of two, twain, twofold, double, two-by-two," from Proto-Germanic *twisnjaz "double" (source also of Old Norse tvinnr "double, twin," Old Danish tvinling , Dutch tweeling , German zwillung ), from PIE *dwisno- (source also of Latin bini "two each," Lithuanian dvynu "twins"), from *dwi- "double," from root *dwo- "two." Minneapolis and St. Paul in Minnesota have been the Twin Cities since 1883, but the phrase was used earlier of Rock Island and Davenport (1856). twin (v.) late 14c., "combine two things closely, join, couple," from twin (adj.). Related: Twinned ; twinning . Earlier and typically in Middle English the verb meant "part, part with, separate from, estrange (a married couple); be parted in twain," of persons or things, "go separate ways" (mid-13c.), on the notion of making two what was one. Hence Middle English twinning (n.) meant "parting, separation, division." also from late 14c. twin (n.) c. 1300, "one of two children born at a single birth," Middle English twin , earlier itwin , from Old English getwinn "double;" getwinnas "twins, two born at one birth," from the adjective (see twin (adj.)). By late 14c. in reference to animals, also the constellation Gemini ( the Twins ). The general sense of "pair linked together by particular relation or resemblance" is by 1580s.
