Origin and history of whine whine (v.) "make low, protracted sounds expressive of distress, etc.," Middle English whinen , from Old English hwinan "to whiz, hiss, or whistle through the air" (only of arrows), also hwinsian (in reference to the whimpering of dogs), from Proto-Germanic *hwinison (source also of Old High German winison , German winseln ). It is ultimately of imitative origin (compare whinny , Old Norse hvina "to whiz," German wiehern "to neigh," Latin hinnire ). The transitive sense of "utter in a thin, plaintive way" is by 1690s. The meaning "complain in a feeble way" is attested by 1520s. Related: Whined ; whining . Whiner (n.) is by c. 1600 as "one who or that which whines." whine (n.) "low, somewhat shrill, protracted cry; feeble, mean, or undignified complaint;" 1630s, from whine (v.). also from 1630s
