Origin and history of hawk hawk (n.) c. 1300, hauk , earlier havek (c. 1200), from Old English hafoc (West Saxon), heafuc (Mercian), heafoc , "hawk," from Proto-Germanic *habukaz (source also of Old Norse haukr , Old Saxon habuc , Middle Dutch havik , Old High German habuh , German Habicht "hawk"), from PIE root *kap- "to grasp" (source also of Russian kobec "a kind of falcon"). Transferred sense of "militarist" attested from 1956, probably based on its opposite, dove . also from c. 1300 hawk (v.1) "to sell in the open, peddle," late 15c., back-formation from hawker "itinerant vendor" (c. 1400), agent noun from Middle Low German höken "to peddle, carry on the back, squat," from Proto-Germanic *huk- . Related: Hawked ; hawking . Despite the etymological connection with stooping under a burden on one's back, a hawker is technically distinguished from a peddler by use of a horse and cart or a van. also from late 15c. hawk (v.2) "to hunt with a hawk," mid-14c., from hawk (n.). also from mid-14c. hawk (v.3) "to clear one's throat," 1580s, imitative. also from 1580s
