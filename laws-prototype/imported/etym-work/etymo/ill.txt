Origin and history of ill ill (adj.) c. 1200, "morally evil; offensive, objectionable" (other 13c. senses were "malevolent, hurtful, unfortunate, difficult"), from Old Norse illr "evil, bad; hard, difficult; mean, stingy," a word of unknown origin. Not considered to be related to evil . It is attested from mid-14c. as "marked by evil intentions; harmful, pernicious." The sense of "sick, unhealthy, diseased, unwell" is recorded by mid-15c., probably from a use similar to that in the Old Norse idiom "it is bad to me." Slang inverted sense of "very good, cool" is 1980s. In Middle English ill-tunged was "overly talkative." also from c. 1200 ill (v.) early 13c., illen , "do evil to," from ill (adj.). Meaning "speak disparagingly, slander, defame" is from 1520s. Related: Illed ; illing . also from early 13c. ill (adv.) late 12c., ill , ille , "wickedly; with hostility; imperfectly," from ill (adj.). Meaning "not well, poorly" also is from c. 1200. It generally has not shifted to the realm of physical sickness, as the adjective has done. Generally contrasted with well , hence the useful, but now obsolete or obscure illcome (1570s), illfare (c. 1300), and illth . Ill-fated is recorded from 1710; ill-informed from 1824; ill-tempered from c. 1600; ill-starred from c. 1600. Ill-disposed "wicked-minded, inclined to oppose or refuse" is from early 15c. also from late 12c. ill (n.) mid-13c., ille , "evil, wrong, wickedness, depravity," c. 1300, "misfortune, calamity," from ill (adj.). also from mid-13c.
