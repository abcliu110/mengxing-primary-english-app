Origin and history of class class (n.) c. 1600, "group of students," in U.S. especially "number of pupils in a school or college of the same grade," from French classe (14c.), from Latin classis "a class, a division; army, fleet," especially "any one of the six orders into which Servius Tullius divided the Roman people for the purpose of taxation;" traditionally originally "the people of Rome under arms" (a sense attested in English from 1650s), and thus akin to calare "to call (to arms)." This is reconstructed to be from PIE root *kele- (2) "to shout" (Watkins). In early use in English also in Latin form classis . The meaning "an order or rank of persons, a number of persons having certain characteristics in common" is from 1660s. School and university sense of "course, lecture" (1650s) is from the notion of a form or lecture reserved to scholars who had attained a certain level. The natural history sense "group of related plants or animals" is from 1753. The meaning "high quality" is from 1874. The sense of "a division of society according to status" (with upper , lower , etc.) is attested by 1763. Class-consciousness (1903) is from German Klassenbewusst . The fault, the evil, in a class society is when privilege exists without responsibility and duty. The evil of the classless society is that it tends to equalize the responsibility, to atomize it into responsibility of the whole population—and therefore everyone becomes equally irresponsible. [T.S. Eliot, BBC interview with Leslie Paul, 1958] also from c. 1600 class (v.) 1705, "to divide into classes, place in ranks or divisions," from class (n.) or French classer . Sense of "to place into a class" is from 1776. Related: Classed ; classing . also from 1705
