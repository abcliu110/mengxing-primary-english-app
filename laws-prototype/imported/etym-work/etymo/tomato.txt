Origin and history of tomato tomato (n.) 1753, "glossy, fleshy fruit of a garden vegetable native to South America," earlier tomate (c. 1600), from Spanish tomate (mid-16c.) from Nahuatl (Aztecan) tomatl "a tomato," said to mean literally "the swelling fruit," from tomana "to swell." Spelling probably influenced by potato (1565). A member of the nightshade family, all of which contain poisonous alkaloids. Introduced in Europe from the New World, by 1550 they were consumed in Italy but in England they were grown only as ornamental plants and the fruits were not eaten there or in the U.S. The Chambers's Cyclopedia supplement of 1753 describes it as "a fruit eaten either stewed or raw by the Spaniards and Italians and by the Jew families of England." Introduced in U.S. 1789 as part of a program by then-Secretary of State Thomas Jefferson, but not commonly eaten there until after c. 1830. The older English name for it, and the usual one before mid-18c., was love-apple (q.v.). The slang meaning "an attractive girl" is by 1929, presumably on the notion of juicy plumpness. Tomato sauce "preparation made from tomatoes and used in dressing meat, etc." is by 1806. Tomato paste is attested by 1938, tomato juice is attested by 1844; its popularity dates from c. 1930 when it began to be touted as a healthy food. Tomato soup (spelled tomata ) is by 1811. also from 1753
