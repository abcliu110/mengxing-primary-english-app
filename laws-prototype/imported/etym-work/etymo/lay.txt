Origin and history of lay lay (v.) "to cause to lie or rest," Middle English leien , from Old English lecgan "to place on the ground (or other surface); place in an orderly fashion," also "put down" (often by striking), from Proto-Germanic *lagojanan . This is reconstructed to be from PIE root *legh- "to lie down, lay." This is the causative form of the ancient Germanic verb that became modern English lie (v.2). The meaning "have sex with" is recorded by 1934, in U.S. slang, probably from sense of "bring forth and deposit" (which was in Old English, as in lay an egg, lay a bet , etc.), perhaps reinforced by to lie with , a phrase frequently met in the Bible. To lay for (someone) "await a chance at revenge" is from late 15c. To lay low "stay inconspicuous" is from 1839; to lay (someone) low "defeat, humble, kill" (late 14c.) preserves the secondary Old English sense. To lay aside (transitive) is by late 14c., "to put to one side or out of the way for a time or for a purpose," also from Middle English as "put away permanently, give up." Germanic cognates include Old Saxon leggian , Old Norse leggja , Old Frisian ledza , Middle Dutch legghan , Dutch leggen , Old High German lecken , German legen , Gothic lagjan "to lay, put, place." lay (adj.) "uneducated, non-professional; non-clerical," early 14c., lai , from Old French lai "secular, not of the clergy" (12c., Modern French laïque ), from Late Latin laicus , from Greek laikos "of the people," from laos "(the common) folk, the people, the crowd; the military; a tribe," in the New Testament especially "the Jewish people," also "the laity," a word of unknown origin. Beekes writes that it is "most often connected with" Hittite lahh- "campaign" and Old Irish laech "warrior," but that the form "is rather Pre-Greek, and has a Pre-Greek suffix -it(o)- . In Middle English, contrasted with learned , a sense revived 1810 in contrast to expert . Laic is a more modern borrowing directly from Late Latin. also from early 14c. lay (n.1) "short song," mid-13c., lai , from Old French lai "song, lyric," a word of unknown origin. Perhaps from Celtic (compare Irish laid "song, poem," Gaelic laoidh "poem, verse, play") because the earliest verses so called were Arthurian ballads, but OED (1989) finds this "out of the question" and prefers a theory which traces it to a Germanic source, such as Old High German leich "play, melody, song." also from mid-13c. lay (n.2) 1550s, "act of laying," from lay (v.). It is attested from 1580s as "a wager." The meaning "relative position, direction, etc.; way in which something is laid" (as in lay of the land ) is recorded by 1819. To take t he lay of the land "observe the state of things generally" (by 1843, American English) is a figure supposedly from a nautical expression but the notion might be rather that in bird's eye view . The slang meaning "line of business" is attested from 1707. The meaning "woman perceived as available for sex" is attested from 1930, but there are suggestions of it in stage puns from as far back as 1767. also from 1550s
