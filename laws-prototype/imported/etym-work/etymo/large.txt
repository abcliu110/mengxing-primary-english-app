Origin and history of large large (adj.) c. 1200 (perhaps late Old English), of persons, "inclined to give or spend freely," also of gifts, "generous, lavish," of areas, "great in expanse." It is from Old French large "broad, wide; generous, bounteous" (12c.), from Latin largus "abundant, copious, plentiful; bountiful, liberal in giving, generous" (source also of Spanish largo "long," Italian largo "wide"), a word of unknown origin. In English it is attested by mid-13c. as "ample in quantity, abundant;" late 14c. as "great in number." Of immaterial things (difference, power, etc.), "ample in range or extent, comprehensive," mid-14c. In reference to things said or written, "long, drawn-out, treated in detail," early 14c. In mod.Eng., a general designation for considerable magnitude, used instead of great when it is not intended to convey the emotional implication now belonging to that word. [OED, 1989] Large-minded is by 1725: "having a liberal or generous mind, marked by breadth of view, not narrow in ideas." Large-hearted is by 1630s ( large-heartedness ), "having a liberal disposition, generous, magnanimous." Large-handed has meant both "grasping, greedy" (c. 1600) and "generous, liberal" (1620s), and also "having large hands" (1885). Adjective phrase larger-than-life is attested by 1840 ( bigger than life is from 1640s). Living large is a modern colloquial expression (by 1994 in African-American vernacular), but large in the sense of "prodigal, lavish" is attested from late 14c. and, of circumstances, "comfortable, easy" from 1738. In more recent use Farmer and Henley ("Slang and Its Analogues") have large (adv.) as "impressively, to excess" from 1852. An older noun sense of "freedom from prison or restraining influence" is preserved in at large "at (one's) liberty" (late 14c.), for which see large (n.). also from c. 1200 large (adv.) mid-14c., "generously, bountifully;" early 15c. as "amply, fully;" late 14c. as "at length, in great detail;" from large (adj.). In nautical use, "before the wind" by 1889. also from mid-14c. large (n.) "liberality, generosity," late 14c., from large (adj.), in phrases involving notions of liberty or freedom, as at large, at one's large , "not subject to imprisonment, confinement, bonds, etc., free to move about in the open." At large also could mean "far and wide" (late 14c.), "in full detail" (1400). The phrase, with the meaning "free or at liberty in a general way" (without particulars) is attested from 1620s; specifically of electors by 1741, American English. also from late 14c. large (v.) mid-14c., largen , "extend, expand" something, also "increase" a number; late 14c. as "enlarge" (a hole, wound, etc.); from large (adj.) and in part an aphetic form of enlarge . Related: Larged ; larging . also from mid-14c.
