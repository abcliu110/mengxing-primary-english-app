Origin and history of doll doll (n.) 1550s, Doll , an endearing name for a female pet or a mistress, from the familiar form of the fem. proper name Dorothy (q.v.). The -l- for -r- substitution in nicknames is common in English: compare Hal for Harold, Moll for Mary, Sally for Sarah , etc. It is attested from 1610s in old slang in a general sense of "sweetheart, mistress, paramour;" by 1640s it had degenerated to "slattern." The sense of "a child's toy baby" is attested by 1700. The sense returned to living beings by 1778 as "pretty, silly woman." By mid-20c. it was used again in slang as an endearing or patronizing name for a young woman. also from 1550s doll (v.) 1867, in dialect, "to pet, indulge" (a child), from doll (n.). Usually with up (adv.). The colloquial meaning "to dress (up)" is attested by 1882 in Australia/New Zealand (e.g. "the young women of this district cannot call themselves ladies until they go to Sydney and come back with their hair "dolled up" in front." Illawarra Daily Mercury, Aug. 7, 1883). When a "lady's" dolled up and a-doing the block, I'll allow she's a starling creation, With her powder and paint; yet her feelings you'll shock, If by chance you mistake her vocation. [Queensland Figaro, Sept. 8, 1888] Related: Dolled ; dolling . also from 1867
