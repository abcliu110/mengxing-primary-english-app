Origin and history of wet wet (adj.) Old English wæt "covered with or permeated by moist, fluid substance; moist, rainy, liquid," also as a noun, "moisture; liquid drink," from Proto-Germanic *wet- (source also of Old Frisian wet ). Also in part from cognate Old Norse vatr , ultimately from PIE root *wed- (1) "water; wet." Of paint, ink, etc., "not yet dry" from 1510s. In medicine, designating diseases characterized by moist secretions, by 1560s. Suggestive of drinking or drunkenness by 1590s; as opposed to dry in reference to the state-by-state U.S. political battles over permitting sales of liquor, from 1870. All wet "in the wrong" is recorded from 1923, American English; earlier simply wet "ineffectual" (1896), and perhaps ultimately from slang meaning "drunken" (c. 1700). The diver's wet-suit is from 1955. Wet dream is from 1851; in the same sense Middle English had ludificacioun "an erotic dream." ["Alphabet of Tales," c. 1450] wet (v.) Middle English weten, from Old English wætan , Mercian wetan , "to wet, moisten, water; be or become wet;" see wet (adj.). From mid-15c. as "to intoxicate" (oneself). To wet one's whistle is by late 14c. The meaning "urinate" is by 1925; earlier "void urine in" (clothes, bed, etc.), 1767. To wet one's pants as an image of panic is by 1967. Related: Wetted ; wetting . wet (n.) Middle English wet, "water as a natural substance; dampness; spell of wet weather," from Old English wæt "that which makes wet" (see wet (adj.)). As "opponent of Prohibition," by 1931.
