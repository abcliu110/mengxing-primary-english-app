Origin and history of anchor anchor (n.) "device for securing ships to the ground under the water by means of cables," Old English ancor , borrowed 9c. from Latin ancora "an anchor," which is from or cognate with Greek ankyra "an anchor, a hook," from PIE root *ang-/*ank- "to bend" (see angle (n.)). A very early borrowing into English and said to be the only Latin nautical term used in the Germanic languages (German Anker , Swedish ankar , etc.). The unetymological -ch- emerged late 16c., a pedantic imitation of a corrupt spelling of the Latin word. The figurative sense of "that which gives stability or security" is from late 14c. The meaning "host or presenter of a TV or radio program" is from 1965, short for anchorman (q.v.). anchor (v.) "fix or secure in a particular place," c. 1200, perhaps in Old English, from anchor (n.) or from Medieval Latin ancorare . Figurative use from 1580s; in reference to television or radio programs, 1961. Related: Anchored ; anchoring . also from c. 1200
