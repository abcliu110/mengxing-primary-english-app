Origin and history of upstairs upstairs (adv.) 1590s, "in or to an upper story; so as to ascend a flight of stairs," from the verbal phrase; see up (adv.) + stairs (under stair ). As an adjective from 1782, "situated on an upper story." The noun, "upper story, part of a building above the ground floor," is attested by 1872. Adjectival meaning "characteristic of upstairs life" (in private rooms of a household, as opposed to servants' quarters) it is recorded from 1942. He [Halifax] had said he had known many kicked down stairs, but he never knew any kicked up stairs before. [Gilbert Burnet, supplement to "History of My own Time," from his original memoirs, c. 1697] The Man Upstairs , colloquial for "God," is from 1943, American English, from a widely reprinted (with variations) World War II story: "The man upstairs brought me down," said Co-Pilot [Lt. Matthew] Vinson, as reported in the Saturday Evening Post. "I talked to Him. I talked to Him plenty and He must have heard me," added the Lieutenant.
