Origin and history of tonic tonic (adj.1) 1640s, "relating to or characterized by muscular tension," from Greek tonikos "of stretching," from tonos "a stretching" (from PIE root *ten- "to stretch"). The meaning "maintaining the healthy firmness of tissues; increasing the strength or tone of the system" is recorded from 1680s. It was extended from 1756 to "bracing, invigorating" on the notion of "having the property of restoring to health." Also extended to mental, moral health. Related: Tonical (1580s). also from 1640s tonic (n.1) "a tonic medicine, remedy which improves tone or vigor of the muscular fibers," especially those of the stomach and bowels, 1799, from tonic (adj.). From 1873 (in gin and tonic ) as short for tonic water (1861 as a commercial product, water infused with quinine), so called because it was held to aid digestion and stimulate appetite. also from 1799 tonic (n.2) in the musical sense, "key-note," 1806, short for tonic note (1760), from tone (n.) in the musical sense + -ic . As "principal key of a composition or passage" by 1896. also from 1806 tonic (adj.2) 1760, "of, pertaining to, or based on the key note;" see tonic (n.2). also from 1760
