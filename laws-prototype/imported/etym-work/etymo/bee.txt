Origin and history of bee bee (n.) stinging insect of the genus Apis , living in societies under a queen and producing wax and honey, Old English beo "bee," from Proto-Germanic *bion (source also of Old Norse by , Old High German bia , Middle Dutch bie ), from PIE root *bhei- "bee." Used metaphorically for "busy worker" since 1530s. The sense of "meeting of neighbors to unite their labor for the benefit of one of their number" is attested by 1769, American English, probably from comparison to the combined labor and social activity of the insect: Raising-bee (1814) for building construction; quilting bee (by 1824, see quilt (v.)); logging-bee for a log-rolling; paring-bee for preparing harvested apples; husking bee (by 1830); also hanging bee "a lynching" (1884). It was extended to other collective situations, as in spelling bee , "contest between two or more for superiority in spelling," which is attested by 1865; spelling school in this sense is attested by 1828; spelling contest is implied by 1825; spelling match attested by 1809. To have a bee in (one's) bonnet (1825), of one who is harebrained or has an intense new notion or fancy, is said in Jamieson to be Scottish, perhaps from earlier expressions such as head full of bees (1510s), denoting mad mental activity.
