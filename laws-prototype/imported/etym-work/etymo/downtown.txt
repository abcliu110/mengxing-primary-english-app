Origin and history of downtown downtown 1787 as an adverb, "into the town," from the prepositional phrase; see down (adv.) + town . In "Poulson's American Daily Advertiser," April 25, in testimony from a trial for a robbery in Philadelphia "She told me the name was Freeman, and they lived down town." It also was used of Philadelphia in the "Aurora" in 1797, 1799. By 1780 in reference to Savannah, by 1800 of Washington, by 1813 of New York. The notion seems to be originally literal, of suburbs built on heights around a port city. By 1814 as an adjective; by 1851 as a noun. Compare uptown . Henry Meigs, of New York, during the squabbling over Missouri. beseeched fellow congressmen in a much-quoted speech to consider "that chapter of human learning which treats of prejudices and sectional feelings, more especially the full history of the factions of the up town and down town boys " [speech printed in National Intelligencer July 19, 1820]. "This city is most delightfully situated and founded on an airy elegant plan ; the public buildings are also equal to my expectations :—But would you think that the uptown and downtown interests had already formed the people, about 4500 in number, into violent parties!" ["Extract of a letter from Washington City," Pennsylvania Republican, May 21, 1800] also from 1787
