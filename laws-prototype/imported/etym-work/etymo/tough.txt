Origin and history of tough tough (adj.) Middle English tough , from Old English toh "strong and firm in texture, flexible without being brittle; tenacious, sticky, not easily separated;" from Proto-Germanic *tanhu- (source also of Middle Low German tege , Middle Dutch taey , Dutch taai , Old High German zach , German zäh ), which Watkins suggests is from PIE *denk- "to bite," on the notion of "holding fast." See rough for the spelling. It is attested from c. 1200 as "strong, powerful," also, in reference to battle or contests, "stiff, severe, violent." It is from c. 1300 as "not tender or fragile, capable of great physical endurance, not easily overcome," and early 14c. as "difficult to chew," also "hard to endure." The figurative sense of "steadfast, not easily influenced, unyielding" is from mid-14c.; that of "hard to do, requiring great or continued effort, laborious" is from 1610s. As "resolute in dealing with opposition," by 1906; to get tough is by 1938, and get-tough as an adjective (with policy , etc.) is by 1978. Tough guy is attested from 1901. Tough-minded is recorded by 1907 (in William James). Of circumstances, implying hardship, injustice, etc., American English colloquial, attested by 1890. Tough luck is attested by 1912; tough shit , dismissive retort to a complaint, is attested by 1946. tough (n.) "street ruffian, bully, person given to violent behavior," 1866, American English, from tough (adj.). Compare rough (n.). also from 1866 tough (v.) in tough it "endure the experience," by 1830, American English colloquial, from tough (adj.). Rough it "put up with hardships" is from 18c.
