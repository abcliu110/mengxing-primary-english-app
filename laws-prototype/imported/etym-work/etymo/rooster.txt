Origin and history of rooster rooster (n.) "cock, male of the domestic hen," 1772, agent noun from roost (v.); earlier roost cock , c. 1600, in sense of "the roosting bird." Said to have become favored in the U.S. (where it was noted by 1820s as a Yankeeism) and said to have been originally a puritan alternative to cock (n.) after that word had acquired the secondary sense "penis" (and compare roach ). But the two words were used interchangeably in New England newspaper articles through mid-19c., and both were popular political symbols. The notion that it was improper to use cock of a male hen is attested by 1850. And it is next to impossible for an Englishman to avoid shocking [American women], if he talks much in their hearing. He must never say anything about the males of fowls, without using some new or round-about form of expression. He may talk about hens , but if he should use the old English form in speaking of the hens' husbands , he would probably find that he had committed an unpardonable offense. Even the use of such a word as 'cock-fighting' would be too much for the mock or morbid modesty of many. HENS are allowed to be hens , but cocks must always be roosters . ["Extract from Jacob Barker's journal," reprinted in various U.S. newspapers 1850. He notes that, for peacock , "male of the pea-fowl" was considered preferable to pea-rooster. ] also from 1772
