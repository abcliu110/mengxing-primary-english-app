Origin and history of wall wall (n.) Middle English wal "upright, enclosing structure around a town, castle, etc.," usually of stone and defensive but also marking its boundary; from Old English weall , Anglian wall , "rampart, dike, earthwork" (natural as well as man-made), "dam, cliff, rocky shore," also "defensive fortification around a city, side of a building." This is an Anglo-Frisian and Saxon borrowing of Latin vallum "strong, defensive wall, rampart of earth and stone" held up by a palisade," also "row or line of stakes." Perhaps a collective form of vallus "stake," which is possibly (Watkins) from a PIE *walso- "a post." De Vaan has vallus as a derivative of vallum and mentions Greek words for "nail" as possible cognates, "which is possible but not compelling." The meaning "interior partition of a house, church, or other structure" emerged by c. 1200. English uses one word where many languages have two, such as German Mauer "outer wall of a town, fortress, etc.," used also in reference to the former Berlin Wall, and wand "partition wall within a building" (compare the distinction, not always rigorously kept, in Italian muro / parete , Irish mur / fraig , Lithuanian mūras / siena , etc.). A Latin word for "defensive wall" was murus (see mural ). Also from the Latin word are Old Saxon, Old Frisian, Middle Low German, Middle Dutch wal . Swedish vall , Danish val are from Low German. Anatomical use is from late 14c. The figurative sense of "barrier, obstacle" was in Old English, as was that, in reference to persons, of "protector, defender." In general of anything or anyone strong, sturdy or steadfast by c. 1300. As a type of something insensate, late 14c. By mid-13c. in reference to the broad, flat, protected upper part of a city wall as a place of pleasant strolls, guard duty, displays of severed heads, etc. To give (someone) the wall "allow him or her to walk on the (cleaner) wall side of the pavement" is from 1530s. To turn (one's) face to the wall "prepare to die" is from 1570s. The phrase up the wall "angry, crazy" is from 1951; off the wall "unorthodox, unconventional" is recorded from 1966, American English student slang. To go over the wall "escape" (originally from prison) is from 1933. wall (v.) "to enclose with a wall," Middle English wallen , from late Old English *weallian (implied in geweallod ), from the source of wall (n.). Also in some cases probably from the Middle English noun. The surname Waller "builder of walls" is attested from early 13c. The meaning "fill up (a doorway, etc.) with a wall" is from c. 1500. The meaning "shut up in a wall, immure" is from 1520s. Related: Walled ; walling .
