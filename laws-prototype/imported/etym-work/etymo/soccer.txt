Origin and history of soccer soccer (n.) 1889, socca , later socker (1891), soccer (1895), originally British university slang (with jocular formation -er (3)), from a shortened form of Assoc. , abbreviation of association in Football Association (as distinguished from Rugby football). An unusual formation, but those who did it perhaps shied away from making a name out of the first three letters of Assoc . Compare rugger (under rugby ), footer , an earlier name for "football" as a sport; also 1890s English schoolboy slang leccer , from lecture (n.), brekkers for breakfast (n.), fresher for freshman . A "Daily Telegraph" article printed in London in August 1899 was widely reprinted in U.S. later that year under "Slang of Oxforders," and called attention to the word: For example, who now speaks of association or Rugby football, and who does not welcome the abbreviated forms of "soccer" and "Rugger?" On whose lips association first changed into "soccer" history is silent. The game was played and so called in U.S. by 1905 when teams there began to compete under Association rules in Ivy League universities and clubs on Philadelphia's Main Line, also a stronghold of cricket. An English all-star team toured the country that year to encourage interest in the sport, and from 1905 the word was used in U.S. to distinguish the kicking game from American football. Average Americans often were puzzled by the words origin, pronunciation, and spelling. One popular explanation was that it was taken from the fact that the players wore heavy woolen socks, and hence the name "socker." [Trenton, N.J. "Times," Dec. 29, 1905] A false etymology that had circulated earlier in England. also from 1889
