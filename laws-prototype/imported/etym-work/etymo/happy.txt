Origin and history of happy happy (adj.) late 14c., "lucky, favored by fortune, being in advantageous circumstances, prosperous;" of events, "turning out well," from hap (n.) "chance, fortune" + -y (2). The sense of "very glad" is recorded by late 14c. The meaning "greatly pleased and content" is from 1520s. Related: Happier ; happiest . Old English had eadig (from ead "wealth, riches") and gesælig , which has become silly . Old English bliðe "happy" survives as blithe . From Greek to Irish, a great majority of the European words for "happy" at first meant "lucky." An exception is Welsh, where the word used first meant "wise." Happy medium "the golden mean" is from 1702. Happy ending in the literary sense is recorded from 1756. Happy hunting ground , the reputed Native American paradise, is attested from 1840, American English. Happy day for "wedding day" is by 1739; happy hour for "early evening period of discount drinks and free hors-d'oeuvres at a bar" is by 1961, said to be 1950s. Happy family , an assemblage of animals of diverse habits and propensities living amicably, or at least quietly, together in one cage.
