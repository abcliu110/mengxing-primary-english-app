Origin and history of traffic traffic (n.) c. 1500, "transportation of merchandise for the purpose of trade;" 1560s in the wider sense of "trade, commerce; interchange of goods, merchandise, or property;" from French trafique (15c.), from Italian traffico (early 14c.), from trafficare "carry on trade." This is a word of uncertain origin; OED (1989) writes that the noun "appears to have been the noun of action from the verb." Perhaps it is from a Vulgar Latin *transfricare "to rub across," from Latin trans "across" (see trans- ) + fricare "to rub" (see friction ), with the original sense of the Italian verb being "touch repeatedly, handle." Or the second element may be an unexplained alteration of Latin facere "to make, do." Klein suggests ultimate derivation of the Italian word from Arabic tafriq "distribution." English spelled the word variously at first; OED (1989) writes that the spelling traffick , "rare before 1600, becomes frequent in the 17th c. and in the form traffic the prevailing one in the 18th c." With suggestion of sinister or illicit dealing by 1660s. The meaning "people and vehicles coming and going, coming and going of persons or transportation of goods along a line of travel" is recorded by 1825. By 1858 as "amount of business done by a railroad in passengers and goods." The word emerges from c. 1915 in city planning discussions of automobiles ( traffic engineer ), earlier it was used in reference to police work on city streets ( traffic-regulating policeman is by 1901). Traffic jam "condition in which traffic on the road cannot proceed" is by 1908, ousting earlier traffic block (1895). Traffic circle is from 1938 (in Britain generally a roundabout ); traffic-island is by 1931; traffic-light by 1929 ( traffic-regulation light is from 1920). also from c. 1500 traffic (v.) 1540s, intransitive, "to buy and sell, pass goods and commodities from one person to another for equivalents or money," from traffic (n.) and preserving the original commercial sense. In an opprobrious or disparaging sense, suggesting illicit, secret, or improper dealing, by 1560s. Related: Trafficked ; trafficking . The -k- is inserted to preserve the "k" sound of -c- before a suffix beginning in -i- , -y- , or -e- (compare picnic / picnicking , panic / panicky , shellac / shellacked ). also from 1540s
