Origin and history of twice twice (adv.) "two times, on two occasions, in two instances," Middle English twies , a late Old English variant of twiga , twigea "two times," from Proto-Germanic *twiyes (source also of Old Frisian twia , Old Saxon tuuio ), from PIE *dwis- , adverbial form of root *dwo- "two." With adverbial genitive -s ; the spelling with -ce reflects the voiceless pronunciation. Twice-told was used by Lydgate as "counted twice" (early 15c.); sense of "related two times" is from Shakespeare: Life is as tedious as a twice-told tale Vexing the dull ear of a drowsy man. ["King John," III.iv., 1595] Think twice, then speak was an "old Prouerbe" by 1623. At twice , though less common than at once , means "at two distinct times; by two distinct operations." Early Middle English also had the adverb without the suffix, as twie "two times, twice; for the second time, as in twi-wiving "bigamy." And Old English sometimes used the -s on the original form, yielding twiges .
