Origin and history of ingenious ingenious (adj.) early 15c., "intellectual, talented," from Old French ingenios , engeignos "clever, ingenious" (Modern French ingénieux ), from Latin ingeniosus "of good natural capacity, full of intellect, clever, gifted with genius," from ingenium "innate qualities, ability; inborn character," literally "that which is inborn," from in- "in" (from PIE root *en "in") + gignere "to beget" (from suffixed form of PIE root *gene- "give birth, beget"). The sense of "skillful, crafty, apt at inventing, clever at contrivance" is recorded by 1570s; earlier in this sense was Middle English enginous (mid-14c.), from Old French engeignos . By 1540s in reference to things, actions, "marked or characterized by inventive genius, displaying skill in contrivance or construction." Middle English also had engineful "skillful (in war)" (c. 1300). By a direct path, Latin ingenium produced Middle English ingeny "intellectual capacity, cleverness" (early 15c.), but this is obsolete. Compare engine . Related: Ingeniously ; ingeniousness . Samuel Daniel used ingeniate (v.) "to contrive, plan" (1590s). Ingeniosity "quality of being ingenious" is by c. 1600, from French ingéniosité or directly from Medieval Latin ingeniositas , from Latin ingeniosus . also from early 15c.
